# Made to Stick: The Complete Framework Reference

**A specialist guide for optimizing writing and communication using the SUCCESs framework.**

---

## Part 1: Origin, Philosophy, and the Curse of Knowledge

### Why This Framework Exists

Chip and Dan Heath wrote *Made to Stick* (2007) to answer a deceptively simple question: **Why do some ideas survive in people's memories and shape behavior, while others evaporate?**

They were troubled by a core observation: most communication fails. Marketing campaigns are forgotten. Presentations don't persuade. Emails aren't acted on. Memos sit unread. The problem isn't that the communicator lacks skill or the audience lacks intelligence - it's that the structure of the message itself is broken.

The Heaths weren't building from theory alone. They started with ethnographic and anecdotal research: studying folklore, urban legends, advertising, medical messaging, and organizational communication. They noticed a pattern. Ideas that stuck - whether a local legend, a viral ad, or a successful health campaign - shared consistent, repeatable characteristics. These weren't traits of great writing. They were structural elements.

Then they mapped this back to cognitive science and memory research, anchoring each element to academic foundations:
- **George Loewenstein's curiosity gap theory** (Unexpected)
- **Cognitive psychology on concreteness and memory** (Concrete)
- **Dual-process theory and emotional processing** (Emotional)
- **Schema theory and mental simulation** (Stories)

The result: SUCCESs. Six elements, individually learnable, that transform how ideas take root.

### The Curse of Knowledge: The Core Enemy

The **curse of knowledge** is the central villain of the framework. It states: *Once you know something, you cannot imagine what it was like not to know it.*

This is not a character flaw. It's a cognitive fact. As expertise deepens, the expert becomes blind to the gaps between what they understand implicitly and what others understand explicitly.

**How the curse manifests in writing:**
- You know your product inside out, so you describe it at the wrong level of abstraction.
- You're familiar with industry jargon, so you use it without defining it.
- You see the logical chain of reasoning, but you don't spell out the connections - they're obvious to you.
- You know why something matters; you forget to explain the "why" to the reader.
- You include details that seem essential because you live in the world of the problem - but your reader doesn't.

The curse is why subject matter experts write the worst copy. A cardiologist explaining heart disease will use medical terms. A CMO explaining a marketing platform will use feature-speak. A founder pitching a solution assumes the investor already understands the problem.

**The SUCCESs framework is, fundamentally, a toolkit for breaking the curse of knowledge.**

Each element is a specific antidote:
- **Simple** forces you to cut through jargon and find the core.
- **Unexpected** forces you to signal that something new is coming (breaking the reader's autopilot).
- **Concrete** forces you to replace abstractions with images and sensory detail.
- **Credible** forces you to justify claims rather than assert them.
- **Emotional** forces you to connect to what the reader cares about, not what you think is important.
- **Stories** force you to make ideas experiential, not just informational.

Without this framework, expertise becomes a liability. With it, expertise becomes teachable.

## Part 2: The SUCCESs Framework - Deep Dive on Each Element

### 1. SIMPLE: The Art of Forced Prioritization

**The core principle:** The most powerful ideas are built on simple cores - not simple-minded, but *maximally reduced*. A simple idea is one where you've cut away everything that isn't load-bearing.

#### The Commander's Intent

The best military communicators use a concept called *commander's intent*. An officer doesn't brief soldiers on every detail of a battle plan. Instead, the officer explains the single goal and the reasoning - and trusts soldiers to adapt tactics to changing conditions.

Applied to writing: What is the *one* thing your reader needs to understand or do? That's your commander's intent. Everything else is detail.

**Example:** A landing page for a task management tool.

*Without commander's intent:* "Our platform offers real-time collaboration, integrates with 50+ tools, supports custom workflows, has mobile apps, includes advanced analytics, and uses enterprise-grade security."

*With commander's intent:* "Stop losing work across 15 apps. Capture ideas in one place, organize them together, and actually ship them."

The second version has prioritized ruthlessly. It doesn't hide features - they're implied by the promise. But the reader first understands the human problem.

#### Finding the Core: Proverbs as Templates

The Heaths point to proverbs as the ultimate example of simplicity. "A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush." "Don't put all your eggs in one basket." "The early bird catches the worm."

These aren't taglines. They're distillations of complex ideas into memorable cores. And they work because they're built on **schemas**-mental structures your brain already has.

Schemas are predictable patterns. A bird is a possession. A hand is security. A bush is uncertainty. When you activate an existing schema, you don't need to explain it. The listener's brain does the work.

**Practical application:** When you're writing, ask:
- What schema does my core idea activate?
- Can I express the core in a form that feels like a proverb - universal, timeless, pattern-based?
- Have I removed every word that isn't earning its place?

#### The Curse of Knowledge in Simplicity

Experts fail at simplicity because they mistake complexity for depth. They include seven reasons when one reason - the true reason - would suffice. They explain the mechanism when they should explain the benefit.

**Before (curse of knowledge):** "Our AI engine uses transformer-based neural networks trained on proprietary datasets to generate contextually relevant content variations, optimizing for semantic diversity and audience segmentation alignment."

**After (simple):** "Write once, generate dozens of versions optimized for different audiences."

The second version doesn't dumb down the idea. It prioritizes what matters to the reader over what matters to the engineer.

### 2. UNEXPECTED: Creating the Knowledge Gap

**The core principle:** People pay attention to novelty and contradiction. Your message needs to first disrupt the reader's autopilot, then fill the gap you've created.

#### George Loewenstein's Curiosity Gap Theory

Psychologist George Loewenstein discovered that curiosity arises when we feel a gap between what we know and what we want to know. This gap creates an itch - and closing it feels rewarding.

Most writing ignores this. It leads with the answer. "Here are five ways to improve email open rates." The reader already expects email tips. No gap. No curiosity.

**Unexpected** means you *announce* the gap before you close it:
- "Most marketers do X. That's backwards."
- "You've probably never thought about Y. But it explains why Z happens."
- "The common wisdom says A. Research shows B instead."

The reader's brain now has a mismatch. Their existing model is shaken. That shaking is attention.

#### The Post-Lead Technique from Journalism

Investigative journalists use a structure called the "post-lead":
1. Deliver a surprising fact or contradiction.
2. Make the reader want to know more.
3. Then, systematically fill the gap.

**Example from a LinkedIn post:**

> "99% of marketing AI tools are built for enterprises. The best AI writing I've seen comes from a solo marketer using ChatGPT and a notebook. Why?"

The reader now *wants* to know. You've created genuine curiosity, not manipulated it.

#### The Difference Between Gimmick Surprise and Structural Surprise

A gimmick surprise is shock for its own sake: "This one weird trick." "You won't believe what happened next." These are novelty without substance. They get attention but don't stick because once the surprise fades, there's no depth.

**Structural surprise** is built into the architecture of the idea itself. It's a genuine contradiction or gap that the rest of the message resolves.

**Example of gimmick surprise:** "Stop doing email marketing! (Just kidding - do it, but do it this way.)" This creates false tension.

**Example of structural surprise:** "Email is dead. Except it's not - it's the only channel where your audience wants to hear from you without algorithms deciding who sees it. That's why X company still makes 40% of revenue from email." The surprise here emerges from the real contradiction between the perception and the reality.

#### Why Unexpected Breaks the Curse of Knowledge

Experts don't realize when they're being obvious. "Of course, you need to understand your customer." To an expert, this is foundational. To the reader encountering it fresh, it might be a gap in their model.

By using Unexpected deliberately, you signal: "Stop coasting on auto-pilot. Something you don't expect is coming."

### 3. CONCRETE: Making Ideas Stick Through Sensory Experience

**The core principle:** Abstract ideas evaporate from memory. Concrete images stick. The brain remembers what it can see, hear, touch, and feel.

#### The Velcro Theory of Memory

Here's how memory actually works: Abstract ideas have few "hooks." They don't attach well to existing knowledge. Concrete ideas have many hooks - sensory details, specific numbers, vivid language - that let them cling to memory networks.

When you say "poverty," the brain has few hooks. When you say "a 10-year-old walking to school in February without a coat," the image sticks. When you describe the cold - the way fingers go numb, breath becomes visible, the body curls inward - the reader has sensory hooks to attach the idea to.

This is why the Heaths call it the "velcro theory." Concrete images are sticky. Abstractions are slippery.

#### Sensory Language and the "Pomelo" Example

One of the book's most memorable examples is the pomelo.

A student working on a marketing campaign for grapefruit juice described it as "a citrus fruit with vitamin C." This is accurate, abstract, and forgettable.

Then they reframed it: "It's a pink grapefruit. Actually, it's a pomelo - larger, sweeter, less bitter. When you cut it, the juice runs down your hands. The flesh is so juicy that when you bite a segment, it bursts in your mouth, and the juice pools on your tongue."

The second version doesn't use any false claims. It just replaces abstraction with concrete sensory detail. The reader can now *taste* the fruit.

#### Specific Numbers vs. Vague Claims

"Many people die in car accidents" is vague. "100,000 people died in car accidents last year" creates a mental image. Better yet: "If you're in a room of 20 people, statistically, two will die in a car accident before they reach 80."

This is called "analogical reasoning." Specific numbers don't just convey facts - they connect abstract statistics to concrete experience.

The brain resists raw numbers. But when you anchor those numbers to something experiential ("that's one person in every room you walk into"), the number becomes concrete.

#### How Aesop's Fables Work

Aesop's fables are built entirely on this principle. "Slow and steady wins the race" is abstract. But the story of the tortoise and the hare gives you:
- A visual image (the hare sprinting, then napping; the tortoise plodding forward)
- An emotional arc (surprise, comeuppance, vindication)
- A scenario you can mentally rehearse (what happens if you quit trying too hard?)

The fable makes the abstract idea *experiential*. You don't just know the idea; you've watched it play out.

#### Concrete Language in Different Contexts

**For LinkedIn posts:**
- Instead of: "Our tool increases productivity."
- Write: "Team members save 3 hours per week not context-switching between tools. That's 12 hours per person per month. In a 10-person team, that's a full person-week of reclaimed time."

**For email:**
- Instead of: "We have great customer support."
- Write: "Sarah's question at 11 PM on Sunday got a response from our team within 90 minutes. Not a ticket number. An actual solution."

**For landing pages:**
- Instead of: "Scalable infrastructure for growing teams."
- Write: "Wednesday morning, 9 AM. Your product hits capacity. By Friday, you're 3x bigger without a single engineering hour spent on scaling. Your CDN, database, and servers all grew automatically."

#### The Curse of Knowledge in Abstraction

Experts live in abstraction. They think about "user segmentation" and "feature adoption" and "go-to-market strategy." But the reader needs concrete. They need to see it, taste it, touch it.

This is why subject matter experts produce bland marketing copy. They're too abstract because abstraction is their native language.

### 4. CREDIBLE: Building Trust Through Multiple Pathways

**The core principle:** Audiences don't believe what you say just because you say it. Credibility must be built through multiple channels, from external authorities to internal proof to testable claims.

#### The Credibility Spectrum

Credibility isn't binary. It exists on a spectrum, and different types of claims require different credibility strategies.

**Level 1: External Authority**
"Trusted experts agree." This is the lowest-friction credibility pathway, but it's also the weakest for long-term belief. You're outsourcing your credibility to someone else.

Example: "According to McKinsey, AI will generate $15 trillion in value."

This works if the audience trusts McKinsey. But it's fragile - if they don't, it fails entirely.

**Level 2: Internal Statistics & Data**
"Here's the objective reality." This is stronger because it's grounded in observation, not opinion.

Example: "Our customers who use feature X see 2.3x higher retention than those who don't."

This is concrete, specific, and harder to argue with. But it requires the audience to trust both your data gathering and your interpretation.

**Level 3: Vivid Details & Unexpected Specificity**
"The specificity proves the truth." When someone cites a specific number - not a round number - it signals authenticity.

Example: "Customers average 47 minutes per week in the tool" (credible) vs. "Customers average 45 minutes per week" (sounds like a guess) vs. "Customers average an hour a week" (nice but generic).

The Heaths call this "the Sinatra Test"-if you can make it there, you can make it anywhere. Specific details prove you've been there.

**Level 4: Anti-Authority & Contradictory Evidence**
"Even sources that disagree with me prove the point." This is the highest-credibility move because it shows intellectual honesty.

Example: "Traditional marketers will tell you that email is dead. Our competitors' own data proves that email still drives 40% of revenue in SaaS. Even their benchmarks work against them."

By acknowledging the opposing view and using their own evidence against them, you build credibility.

**Level 5: Testable Claims**
"Try it yourself." This removes credibility entirely from the equation. The reader becomes the authority.

Example: "Open your last 10 client emails. Count how many had a concrete example or story vs. just features. I bet fewer than 3 did. That's why they didn't convert."

Now the reader doesn't have to trust you. They can verify the claim themselves. This is the strongest form of credibility because it's self-evident.

#### The Sinatra Test

"If you can make it there, you can make it anywhere."

This concept from Frank Sinatra's era means: if something works in the hardest place (New York City for entertainers), it proves the concept. Applied to credibility, the Sinatra Test means: show that your claim holds even in the toughest test case.

Example: "This productivity tool works for a solo founder working 60 hours a week, which is honestly a terrible use case for any tool. If it works for them, it works for anyone."

By proving the concept in an adverse condition, you've proven the concept universally.

#### Anti-Authority Credibility

Sometimes, credibility comes from *not* being the authority. The Heaths point to cases where an expert claims something surprising *against their own interest*.

Example: "A financial planner admits: most people don't need expensive financial planning. Here's how to do it yourself for free."

This is credible because the financial planner is saying something that costs them money. Why would they say it if it wasn't true?

Similarly: A former skeptic is more credible than a true believer. A competitor who praises you is more credible than your own marketing. A limitation you acknowledge is more credible than a purely positive claim.

#### The Curse of Knowledge in Credibility

Experts assume their claims are self-evident. "Of course this works - I know because I built it." But the reader doesn't know. They need proof. This is where experts fail: they present assertions instead of evidence.

The antidote: Assume the reader is skeptical. Provide evidence from the reader's frame of reference, not your expertise.

### 5. EMOTIONAL: Identity, Not Interests

**The core principle:** Most messages fail emotionally because they appeal to interests ("this will help your business") instead of identity ("this is who you are"). Emotions aren't added on top of ideas - they're the foundation.

#### The "What's in It for Me?" Trap

Most marketing starts with interest-based appeals:
- "This software saves time" (appeals to efficiency interest)
- "This investment returns 15%" (appeals to wealth interest)
- "This diet loses 20 pounds" (appeals to health interest)

These are logical. They're rational. And they're rarely enough to move people to action.

The Heaths point to research showing that interest-based appeals create shallow engagement. The audience hears the message, understands it, forgets it. There's no emotional hook.

#### Maslow vs. Herzberg: A Counterintuitive Insight

Maslow's hierarchy of needs (physiological → safety → belonging → esteem → self-actualization) suggests that you motivate people by meeting unmet needs.

But Herzberg's research on motivation in the workplace flipped this. He found that *satisfying basic needs doesn't motivate*. Not having food makes you miserable. But having food doesn't make you motivated - it just makes you not-miserable.

**The implication:** Interests work this way. "This software saves time" is like saying "you'll have food." It's table stakes. But it's not motivating.

What motivates is *identity*. Appeals that say, "This is who you are. This is who you want to be. Use this to become that."

#### Identity-Based vs. Interest-Based Appeals

**Interest-based:** "This exercise program builds muscle" or "This writing tool helps you write faster."

**Identity-based:** "Real writers ship. They don't wait for inspiration. They sit down and write, even when it's messy. This tool is for writers who write." or "People who care about their health don't skip workouts - they schedule them and show up. Here's your scheduling tool."

The identity-based version presumes that the reader already *identifies* as a writer or as someone who cares about health. It's not trying to convince them; it's recognizing who they already are and offering a tool for that identity.

This is more emotionally resonant because it touches something deeper: self-image, belonging, aspiration.

#### The Mother Teresa Effect

The Heaths describe the "Mother Teresa effect": people are moved by *one person's story* more than by statistics about millions.

Show a statistic: "3 million children die of malaria each year."
Audience response: sad, abstractly concerned, paralyzed.

Tell a story: "Cynthia is four years old. She has malaria. Her family has no money for treatment. She'll likely be dead within weeks unless..."
Audience response: engaged, motivated to help.

Why? Because the brain can't hold a million people emotionally. It can hold one person's face, name, and story. Emotional engagement happens at the individual level.

This is crucial for writing. Abstract appeals ("help the needy") fail. Specific appeals ("meet this one child") work.

#### Emotional Layering: Fear, Hope, Identity, Status

Different emotions work for different messages:
- **Fear:** Creates urgency but can backfire if the solution isn't clear ("This vulnerability will destroy your data" vs. "This vulnerability will destroy your data. Here's how to fix it in 10 minutes.")
- **Hope:** Elevates the vision but can feel manipulative if not grounded ("This tool will change your life" is weaker than "This tool removes the friction so you can focus on what matters.")
- **Identity:** Creates sustainable engagement ("You're a builder. Builders build. Use this to build faster.")
- **Status/Belonging:** Creates social proof and community ("Join the 10,000+ people building with this tool.")

The most sticky messages use multiple emotional layers, not just one.

#### The Curse of Knowledge in Emotion

Experts are blind to what makes their idea emotionally compelling to an outsider. They see the features. They don't see the identity the customer wants to claim.

A fitness app expert sees "real-time heart rate monitoring." The customer sees "I'm someone who takes their health seriously." The expert's copy emphasizes the feature. It should emphasize the identity.

### 6. STORIES: Mental Simulation and Emotional Truth

**The core principle:** Stories aren't decoration. They're the most powerful format for making ideas sticky because they simulate experience. When you hear a story, your brain doesn't just understand it - it rehearses it.

#### How Stories Work: Simulation Theory

When you read or hear a story, your brain does something remarkable: it *runs a simulation*. The brain activates the same neural regions that would be active if you were actually experiencing the events.

A well-crafted story about climbing a mountain activates motor regions (your brain is climbing), visual regions (you're seeing the landscape), emotional regions (you're feeling the fatigue and triumph).

This is why stories are sticky: your brain has *experienced* the idea, not just heard about it. And experience is more memorable than information.

#### The Three Plot Archetypes

Stories in business contexts typically follow three patterns:

**1. The Challenge Plot**
"Someone faces an obstacle. They overcome it. We learn from their solution."

**Example:** A marketing manager faces a team that's disorganized. She implements a simple structure. The team's output doubles. Lesson: this structure works.

This plot teaches through problem-solution. It's useful for demonstrating that something difficult is achievable.

**2. The Connection Plot**
"Someone reveals a human truth that connects people emotionally."

**Example:** A customer success story where a struggling business owner's team was about to quit, but a simple process change kept them engaged. The deeper lesson: people want to feel like they matter and their work matters.

This plot works because it taps into something universal (the need to feel valued, the fear of losing good people) and makes it concrete and real.

**3. The Creativity Plot**
"Someone sees an opportunity others missed and does something unexpected."

**Example:** A team that was losing customers to a competitor realized the competitor wasn't actually better - just easier to set up. They rebuilt their onboarding. Customers came back. Lesson: what seems like a feature problem is often a usability problem.

This plot teaches by showcasing lateral thinking. It's useful for encouraging people to think differently.

#### Springboard Stories

A **springboard story** is a story from outside your world that illustrates a principle you want to teach - but doesn't directly show your product.

Why? Because if you tell a story where your product is the hero, people get defensive. They assume you're selling. If you tell a story about someone else solving a problem in a surprising way, they let their guard down. They see the principle. Then they can apply it to your world.

**Example (anti-pattern):**
"Our founder started a failing company. He used our platform. The company succeeded. Buy our platform."

**Example (springboard story):**
"A carpenter was losing jobs to bigger contractors. He realized he was worse at sales than they were, not worse at carpentry. He invested in a simple CRM just to track who he'd talked to. His closing rate doubled. Three years later, he's doing what he loves and making more money."

The second story is more powerful because the audience projects themselves into it. They don't feel sold to; they feel understood.

#### Why Stories Activate the Same Brain Regions as Experience

This is neuroscience: when you listen to someone describe their emotional state, your brain activates the regions that would be active if you felt that emotion. When someone describes motion, your motor cortex activates.

This is called *neural coupling*. The storyteller's brain state and the listener's brain state become synchronized.

This is why stories are so powerful in communication. The listener isn't just absorbing information; they're *becoming attuned* to the storyteller's experience.

#### Stories as Vehicles for Identity

The most powerful stories in marketing aren't about the product. They're about who the customer wants to be.

**Example:**
Not: "Our tool has integrations with 50 apps."
But: "A solo founder spent 12 hours per week context-switching between 15 different tools. She's smart. She's organized. But the tools were killing her productivity. Then she discovered a way to automate the connections. Now she spends those 12 hours actually building her business. She's shipping twice as fast."

This story isn't about features. It's about identity: "I'm a builder who deserves to spend my time building, not administrating."

#### The Curse of Knowledge in Stories

Experts often think they don't have good stories. "My industry is boring." In fact, the expertise itself is the problem. An expert inside an industry can't see what's remarkable about it to an outsider.

The solution: tell stories from the *customer's perspective*, not from the insider's perspective. The customer's struggle is remarkable. The expert's solution is obvious.

## Part 3: Diagnosing Which Elements Are Missing

When you're reviewing a piece of writing (your own or someone else's) and something feels weak, you can use this diagnostic framework to identify which SUCCESs elements are absent or underdeveloped.

### The Diagnosis Matrix

| Element | Symptoms of Absence | Diagnostic Questions |
|---------|-------------------|----------------------|
| **Simple** | Jargon, feature-dumping, unclear core message, reader asks "so what?" | Can I state the core in one sentence? Does every sentence earn its place? Am I using industry language I'd need to define? |
| **Unexpected** | Reader feels they've heard this before, no curiosity, predictable flow, autopilot engagement | Did I signal that something novel is coming? Is there a genuine gap between expectation and reality? Does the reader want to keep reading? |
| **Concrete** | Abstract language, vague claims, forgettable details, reader can't visualize it | Can I describe this using sensory language? Do I have specific numbers instead of ranges? Can the reader taste, see, or feel it? |
| **Credible** | Reader thinks "prove it," unsubstantiated claims, sounds like marketing | Have I provided evidence, not assertions? Could a skeptic verify this? Am I using specific details that prove authenticity? |
| **Emotional** | Reader understands but doesn't care, feels like information not motivation, rational but cold | Does this appeal to identity or just interests? Can I see my reader in this? Does it matter to them personally or just professionally? |
| **Stories** | Theoretical, no examples, reader can't imagine what this looks like in practice | Is there a specific scenario, character, or moment? Can the reader mentally rehearse this? Does it teach through simulation or just assertion? |

### Workflow: How to Diagnose Your Own Writing

1. **Read the piece as if you're the target audience, not the author.** What's your natural reaction?
2. **Go through each SUCCESs element in order.** Does this piece have it? How strong is it (weak/medium/strong)?
3. **Identify the 1-2 weakest elements.** These are where your revision effort should go.
4. **Check for false positives.** Make sure you're not confusing "this element is absent" with "this element is done well but subtly."

### Common Diagnostic Patterns

**Pattern 1: Simple + Concrete but Missing Emotional**
This is expert writing that's clear but cold. The reader understands but doesn't care.
*Fix:* Add an identity-based appeal or a story that shows why this matters to someone like them.

**Pattern 2: Emotional + Stories but Missing Credible**
This is engaging but sounds like opinion. The reader feels it but doubts it.
*Fix:* Add specific numbers, details, or a testable claim that proves the story.

**Pattern 3: Simple + Expected**
This is boring. Clear and straightforward but forgettable.
*Fix:* Find a genuine contradiction or gap. Lead with what's surprising.

**Pattern 4: Concrete + Stories but Missing Simple**
This is meandering. Rich detail and examples but no clear point.
*Fix:* State the core idea first. Make it your commander's intent. Let the stories and details support it.

## Part 4: Application Patterns by Content Type

Different content types have different constraints and opportunities. Here's how to apply SUCCESs to each:

### LinkedIn Posts

**Constraints:** Limited attention, rapid scroll, must earn the first line, no deep context assumed.

**SUCCESs Priority:** Unexpected > Emotional > Concrete > Simple > Stories > Credible

**Why this order:**
- You must stop the scroll first (Unexpected).
- Then connect emotionally so they care enough to read on (Emotional).
- Then make it vivid enough to remember (Concrete).
- Then make sure the core is clear (Simple).
- Then tell a story if room allows (Stories).
- Credible is lower priority because LinkedIn audiences already have high credibility for personal stories (you're the authority on your own experience).

**Template:**
```
[Unexpected hook that contradicts the reader's assumption]

[Emotional setup: identity or status appeal]

[Concrete example or story]

[Simple takeaway: the one thing to remember]
```

**Example:**
> Most marketing directors think they're good at email. They're wrong.
>
> I audited 100+ company emails last month. Here's what I found: 94% had no story. No example. No reason to believe anything. Just feature-dumps.
>
> Email readers don't care about your features. They care if you understand their problem. One sentence showing you *get* them beats ten sentences of benefits.
>
> If your next email has a story or example, your open rate will jump. I've seen it with every team I've worked with.

This hits Unexpected (contradiction), Emotional (identity-"you think you're good, but..."), Concrete (specific number: 94%), Simple (one sentence beats ten), and implies credibility through specific observation.

### Email Subject Lines and Body

**Subject line constraints:** 50 characters, must create curiosity or emotional resonance without clickbait.

**Body constraints:** Real estate is limited; every paragraph must earn its place.

**SUCCESs Priority:** Unexpected > Credible > Concrete > Simple > Emotional > Stories

**Why:** The subject line must overcome inbox noise (Unexpected). The body must prove the subject line's claim (Credible) with specific detail (Concrete) before asking for action.

**Example subject line (bad):** "New Feature Update"
**Example subject line (good):** "The #1 reason your team ignores internal tools"

The second creates a knowledge gap. The reader wants to know. It's not clickbait; it's a genuine promise.

**Example body:**
```
Hi [Name],

You've probably noticed that adoption of [tool] has plateaued.

The problem isn't the tool. It's the setup.

Last week, a team I work with went from 30% active users to 71%
by changing one thing: their onboarding.

They stopped explaining features. They started with a real scenario
from the customer's day. Then showed how [tool] fits into that scenario.

Users got it. They adopted.

Can we schedule 15 minutes this week? I want to show you the before/after
and help you do the same.

[Schedule link]

Noy
```

This works because:
- Unexpected: "it's not the tool, it's the setup" contradicts the reader's assumption
- Concrete: specific number (71% vs. 30%), specific scenario
- Credible: the story is detailed enough to be real
- Simple: the core is one sentence ("explain the scenario, not features")

### Landing Pages and Sales Copy

**Constraints:** Must persuade someone who's skeptical, competing with other options, and has limited patience.

**SUCCESs Priority:** Simple > Concrete > Emotional > Stories > Credible > Unexpected

**Why:** Landing page visitors have already decided they might be interested (they clicked the link). You don't need to create curiosity; you need to immediately confirm they made the right choice. Start with the core promise (Simple), make it vivid (Concrete), connect it to identity (Emotional), prove it works (Stories and Credible).

**Example (bad):**
```
Welcome to CloudSync Pro

Our platform offers seamless cloud synchronization
with advanced encryption, real-time collaboration,
and enterprise-grade infrastructure.

Features:
- 256-bit AES encryption
- 99.99% uptime SLA
- Real-time data sync
- Integrations with 50+ tools
```

This is Simple and maybe Concrete (technical specs) but fails because:
- No Emotional connection - who cares about 256-bit AES?
- No Credible proof - why should I believe the 99.99%?
- No Stories - I can't see myself using it.

**Example (good):**
```
Your Team Works Across 15 Tools. Your Data Lives in All of Them.

That's a problem. When Sarah updates the doc, it doesn't hit the project
tracker. When the project tracker updates, it doesn't notify Slack.

By Wednesday, nobody knows what the actual status is.

With CloudSync, when Sarah updates the doc, everything updates:
tracker, Slack, email, calendar. Instantly. No manual sync. No handoff.

One team saw task reassignment cycle time drop from 90 minutes to 15.
They're shipping twice as fast.

See how it works:
[Video]

Start your free trial:
[Button]
```

This works because:
- Simple: "one tool that syncs everything" (clear core)
- Concrete: "15 tools," "90 minutes to 15," "Wednesday," "Sarah"
- Emotional: identity appeal-"you're a builder who shouldn't waste time syncing"
- Credible: specific numbers and a customer result (even if anonymized)
- Stories: the Sarah scenario lets you visualize it

### Internal Communications and Memos

**Constraints:** Audience has limited context, assuming you're asking for something difficult, may be skeptical of change.

**SUCCESs Priority:** Simple > Emotional > Credible > Concrete > Unexpected > Stories

**Why:** Internal comms need clarity first (Simple)-your team needs to understand the change immediately. Then emotional buy-in (Emotional)-why should they care? Then credible proof (Credible)-why will this work? Unexpected is lower priority because internal audiences are usually already paying attention (they have to).

**Example (bad):**
```
Team,

Effective next quarter, we're implementing a new project management
system to improve collaboration and increase productivity.

Please attend the mandatory training session on Tuesday at 2 PM.

Management
```

This fails because:
- No Simple core: "we're changing tools" is not the same as "here's why we're changing tools"
- No Emotional appeal: "you have to attend training" doesn't explain why this benefits them
- No Credible proof: "improve collaboration and increase productivity" are vague assertions

**Example (good):**
```
Team,

Next quarter, we're switching project management systems.

Here's why: right now, you spend 3+ hours per week updating three
different systems so leadership has visibility. That's 30 hours per
month lost to administrative work.

The new system does that updating for you. Automatically.

Three teams piloted it. Average reclaimed time: 2.5 hours per week.

You're not getting more work. You're getting 10 hours per month back to
use on work that matters.

Training is Tuesday at 2 PM. I'll show you how this saves you time.

Management
```

This works because:
- Simple: "we're switching to save you time"
- Emotional: identity appeal-"you deserve to spend time on real work"
- Concrete: specific time numbers (3 hours, 10 hours, 2.5 hours)
- Credible: pilot data from real teams
- Stories implied: the scenario of context-switching

### Presentations and Talks

**Constraints:** Audience is captive but can mentally check out. Visuals matter. Pacing matters.

**SUCCESs Priority:** Stories > Unexpected > Emotional > Concrete > Simple > Credible

**Why:** Live audiences engage most through narrative (Stories). Open with something surprising (Unexpected) to hook attention. Build emotional resonance (Emotional) through specific examples (Concrete). Anchor the core idea (Simple) repeatedly. Credible comes last because your presence and delivery add credibility.

**Structure:**
1. **Open:** An unexpected story or contradiction that creates curiosity
2. **Middle:** Concrete examples that build the idea incrementally, with emotional hooks
3. **Transition:** Restate the simple core idea
4. **Proof:** Show credible data, results, or case studies
5. **Close:** Return to the opening story or theme, elevate the identity

**Example opening (unexpected + story):**
> "I spent three months at a SaaS company trying to make their landing page convert better. We tested colors, copy, layouts, CTAs. We improved the conversion rate by 0.3%. Then the CEO asked a simple question: 'Why are people visiting at all if they're not sure what we do?' We rewrote the headline to answer one question: who is this for and what problem does it solve? Conversion went up 34% without any other changes. Everything we'd done before was optimization. This was transformation."

This works because:
- Unexpected: three months of tweaking, then one change that dwarfs the effort
- Story: specific scenario with a character and moment
- Emotional: appeals to the identity of someone trying to optimize (you're doing it wrong)
- Sets up the core idea: clarity beats optimization

## Part 5: Multi-Element Combinations and Synergies

Not all elements are created equal in every context. But certain combinations are particularly powerful.

### The "Holy Trinity" Combinations

**Combination 1: Simple + Concrete + Emotional**
This is the most robust combination. It works across almost all content types.
- Simple makes the idea understandable
- Concrete makes it memorable
- Emotional makes it motivating

**Example:** "Stop losing ideas in a notebook. Use this app. Your ideas stay where they belong: safe and searchable."

**Combination 2: Unexpected + Stories + Credible**
This is the most persuasive combination. It creates doubt, resolves it with a narrative, and backs it with proof.
- Unexpected creates openness (you're not sure what's coming)
- Stories build belief (you see someone experience it)
- Credible confirms belief (you see the evidence)

**Example (used in landing page):**
"People think remote teams are less productive. A 200-person company proved otherwise. They went fully remote, implemented async-first workflows, and shipped 40% more features in the first year. Here's how they did it. Here's their metrics proving it."

**Combination 3: Stories + Emotional + Concrete**
This is the most memorable combination. It teaches through experience.
- Stories structure the narrative
- Emotional makes it personal
- Concrete makes it specific and real

**Example (LinkedIn):**
"My founder spent 60 hours writing a 20-page proposal. The client skimmed it, asked three questions, and made a decision. Those 60 hours boiled down to three sentences. That's when I realized: good writing isn't about quantity. It's about identifying the three things that matter and explaining only those."

### Rare But Powerful: All Six Elements

When you can hit all six elements, the result is extraordinarily sticky.

**Example:**

> **Why Smart Marketers Quit Using Email Drip Campaigns**
>
> [Unexpected + Simple]: Most drip campaigns are built backward. They start with what you want to say. They should start with what the prospect wants to know.
>
> [Concrete]: Here's what's typical: Day 1, you send "Welcome." Day 3, you send "Here are our features." Day 5, "See how we compare." By day 7, 60% have unsubscribed because none of it answered their question: "Is this for me?"
>
> [Stories + Emotional]: A director at a B2B SaaS company told me this week: "I finally set up a real drip campaign. Instead of sending features, I asked: what's the real problem our customer has? What keeps them up at night? I built the sequence around answering *that*." Her open rate went from 12% to 31%.
>
> [Credible]: Seven teams I've worked with have tried this. Average improvement: 2.3x engagement. It doesn't require new tools. It requires asking one question differently.
>
> [Simple + Emotional]: If you send campaigns, they should answer your prospect's question before you ask them to solve yours.

Let's verify:
- **Unexpected**: "quit using drip campaigns" is attention-getting, especially if drips are the reader's current tactic
- **Simple**: "answer their question before pushing yours"
- **Concrete**: specific numbers (Day 1, Day 3, 60%, 12% to 31%, seven teams)
- **Credible**: specific improvement metric (2.3x), multiple teams
- **Emotional**: appeals to identity (you're a "smart marketer" who cares about results)
- **Stories**: the director's narrative

This example works because each element reinforces the others rather than competing.

### Dangerous Combinations (Avoid)

**High Unexpected + Low Simple**
This creates confusion. "I have your attention but you don't understand the point."
*Fix:* Always follow up Unexpected with Simple. Create the gap, then fill it.

**High Emotional + Low Credible**
This creates skepticism. "You're manipulating me emotionally without backing it up."
*Fix:* When you appeal to emotion, back it with specific credible proof.

**High Stories + Low Simple**
This creates meandering communication. Great examples but unclear why.
*Fix:* State the point before or after the story. Let the story illustrate, not define.

## Part 6: Anti-Patterns and Failure Modes

### Common Mistakes Practitioners Make

**Mistake 1: Confusing Simplicity with Oversimplification**

*Bad approach:* Reducing an idea to nothing.
> "Good writing is short."

This is simple, but it's so oversimplified that it's useless. Short writing can be bad if it lacks substance. The real insight is "writing should cut away everything that isn't load-bearing." That's more complex, but it's actually simpler because it gives you a decision rule.

*Better:* "Good writing has no wasted words. Every sentence earns its place."

This is simple (clear rule) without being simplistic (it actually guides decisions).

**Mistake 2: Forcing Unexpected Where It Doesn't Fit**

*Bad approach:* Making something surprising for its own sake.
> "Internal Memo: We're canceling Casual Friday! (Kidding. We're expanding it.)"

This creates false surprise. The reader feels manipulated.

*Better:* Use Unexpected when there's a genuine contradiction or insight.
> "Casual Friday is reducing productivity. We're making every day casual so people stop context-switching."

This is surprising because it reframes the problem. It's not a trick; it's a genuine insight.

**Mistake 3: Concrete Details That Don't Matter**

*Bad approach:* Including specific numbers that don't illuminate.
> "Our customer Sarah, who was 32 years old and had two kids and lived in Denver..."

These details are concrete but irrelevant. They don't help the reader understand the point.

*Better:* "Sarah had 15 tools open and spent 60% of her day context-switching. When she consolidated to one platform, she reclaimed 12 hours per week."

These details are concrete *and* load-bearing. They illustrate the problem and solution.

**Mistake 4: Credibility Overkill**

*Bad approach:* Citing every possible authority and statistic.
> "According to McKinsey, Gartner, Forrester, and a study from Stanford, AI will increase productivity."

Too many sources reduce credibility. It sounds like you're desperate to convince.

*Better:* One source + one specific data point.
> "McKinsey found that companies using AI saw a 23% productivity increase. But more tellingly: in one team I worked with, a single AI tool eliminated 3 hours per week of busywork."

One external source + one internal observation = more credible than five sources.

**Mistake 5: Emotional Appeal Without Identity**

*Bad approach:* Generic emotional language.
> "This will change your life!"

This is too broad. Everyone says it.

*Better:* Identity-specific appeal.
> "If you're someone who ships, you know that every hour on busywork is an hour you're not building. This gives those hours back."

This recognizes a specific identity ("someone who ships") and appeals to it.

**Mistake 6: Stories That Don't Teach**

*Bad approach:* Stories for entertainment value.
> "So I was at a coffee shop, and this random guy told me about his startup idea..."

Good story, but it doesn't illustrate any principle. It's just narrative.

*Better:* Stories that reveal a principle.
> "A founder I know kept losing customers to competitors. He assumed the competitor had better features. Then he realized: the competitor's onboarding took 5 minutes. His took 30. That's the only difference. One week after fixing onboarding, his churn dropped 40%."

This story teaches: perceived quality often comes from user experience, not features.

### When SUCCESs Elements Backfire

**Simple becomes Reductive**
If you cut too much, your message becomes meaningless.
*Example:* "Buy now." (Simple, but no reason to buy.)

**Unexpected becomes Manipulative**
If the surprise doesn't match the substance, you feel tricked.
*Example:* "Doctors hate this one weird hack." (The "hack" is flossing, which everyone already knows.)

**Concrete becomes Pedantic**
If you include too many details, it becomes overwhelming.
*Example:* Listing every feature when you should list the benefit.

**Credible becomes Defensive**
If you cite too much evidence, you sound uncertain.
*Example:* "Seven studies prove this, and here are ten customer testimonials, and this expert agrees..." (If it's that obvious, why so much defending?)

**Emotional becomes Manipulative**
If you appeal to emotion without substance, it feels like a con.
*Example:* "Imagine yourself rich and free!" (Without showing how the product delivers this.)

**Stories become Irrelevant**
If the story doesn't connect to the point, it's just entertainment.
*Example:* A great story about someone's childhood that has no bearing on your product.

## Part 7: Interaction with Other Writing Frameworks

### Made to Stick vs. StoryBrand (Donald Miller)

**Overlap:** Both prioritize clarity and use stories.

**Key differences:**

| Aspect | Made to Stick | StoryBrand |
|--------|--------------|-----------|
| **Core focus** | Making ideas memorable | Creating customer transformation narrative |
| **Story structure** | Any of three archetypes; flexible | Specific 7-step hero's journey (customer is hero, you're guide) |
| **Audience role** | Varied; can be anyone | Customer is always the protagonist |
| **Primary goal** | Idea adoption | Sales and action |
| **Tone** | Can be any tone; depends on context | Warm, coaching, aspirational |

**When to use Made to Stick:** When you need to communicate an idea that people need to remember and act on over time.

**When to use StoryBrand:** When you're explicitly trying to sell something and need to frame the customer as the hero.

**Synthesis:** A landing page could use StoryBrand's customer-centric story arc while hitting Made to Stick's SUCCESs elements.

### Made to Stick vs. Smart Brevity (Axios)

**Overlap:** Both prioritize clarity and cutting unnecessary words.

**Key differences:**

| Aspect | Made to Stick | Smart Brevity |
|--------|--------------|-----------------|
| **Core focus** | Making ideas sticky across time | Communicating efficiently in the moment |
| **Structure** | Flexible; depends on the element | Rigid: lead → context → takeaway |
| **Length** | Can be long if needed | Always short; often 100 words or less |
| **Goal** | Memory and adoption | Quick comprehension |
| **Tools** | 6 elements to choose from | Specific format to follow |

**When to use Made to Stick:** When you have room to develop an idea and need it to stick in memory.

**When to use Smart Brevity:** When you need to communicate fast and efficiently (internal updates, status reports).

**Synthesis:** A LinkedIn post could use Smart Brevity's structure (lead → context → takeaway) while hitting SUCCESs elements (Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, etc.).

### Made to Stick vs. JTBD Writing (Jobs to Be Done)

**Overlap:** Both focus on customer motivation; both are anti-jargon.

**Key differences:**

| Aspect | Made to Stick | JTBD |
|--------|--------------|------|
| **Focus** | How to structure ideas for memory | Why customers choose solutions |
| **Approach** | Message design | Problem identification |
| **Core question** | "How do I make this sticky?" | "What job is the customer hiring this for?" |
| **Output** | Memorable messaging | Positioning and strategy |

**When to use Made to Stick:** For writing copy that converts or communicates.

**When to use JTBD:** For understanding the customer problem before writing.

**Synthesis:** Use JTBD to identify the real job the customer is hiring you for. Then use Made to Stick's elements to communicate that job in memorable, compelling copy.

### Made to Stick vs. Gary Provost's Rhythm Framework

**Overlap:** Both are about creating engagement through structure.

**Key differences:**

| Aspect | Made to Stick | Provost |
|--------|--------------|---------|
| **Focus** | Content/idea structure | Sentence rhythm and pacing |
| **Scale** | Idea-level (what to say) | Sentence-level (how to say it) |
| **Tool** | 6 content elements | Sentence length variation, flow |
| **Purpose** | Memory and adoption | Readability and engagement |

**When to use Made to Stick:** For deciding what to say and how to structure it.

**When to use Provost:** For the sentence-by-sentence execution (varying sentence length, creating rhythm).

**Synthesis:** Use Made to Stick to decide what to say (Simple core, Concrete details, Stories). Use Provost to make each sentence sing (rhythm, pacing, varied length).

## Conclusion: The Framework as a Practical Tool

The Made to Stick framework is not a formula to apply rigidly. It's a diagnostic toolkit.

When you're writing and something feels weak, you can ask:
- Is this Simple enough that the reader understands the core?
- Is this Unexpected enough that the reader pays attention?
- Is this Concrete enough that the reader can visualize it?
- Is this Credible enough that the reader believes it?
- Is this Emotional enough that the reader cares?
- Is this a Story or does it need to be?

Each element is a lever. You don't need to max out all six. You need to identify which elements are doing the heavy lifting for your particular context and which are weak.

A landing page might rely heavily on Concrete + Emotional. A LinkedIn post might rely on Unexpected + Stories. An internal memo might rely on Simple + Credible.

The curse of knowledge is the real enemy - the gap between what you know and what your reader knows. Every element in SUCCESs is a tool for bridging that gap.

Master this framework, and you'll understand why some writing sticks and some evaporates. More importantly, you'll know how to fix it.
