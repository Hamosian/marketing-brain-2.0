# The critique method

The format-agnostic engine. Every format in `format-playbooks.md` is the same
machine under a different skin: **a promise, a chain of cause-and-effect that
keeps the reader mid-motion, and a payoff that discharges the promise.** Run
these passes in order; the format playbook layers on top.

## Pass 1: Steelman reconstruction

Before judging anything, state what the piece is *trying* to do in its
strongest form: the promise it makes, the belief-change or action it wants, and
the best version of its argument. Attack that, never a strawman. This is also
Pixar's "note behind the note" discipline: diagnose the structural cause, not
the surface symptom. "The intro is slow" is a symptom; the note is usually "the
promise isn't established, so nothing can feel slow *relative to* it."

## Pass 2: Beat map with connective-tissue audit

List the beats, then label the connector between each adjacent pair:

- **BUT** - a complication or reversal. Alive.
- **THEREFORE** - a consequence that follows. Alive (but verify it actually
  follows; a THEREFORE that doesn't follow is a logical hole wearing story
  clothes).
- **AND THEN** - mere sequence. Dead. (The South Park rule: if beats connect
  with "and then," the story is dead at that joint.)

AND-THEN density is the single best universal detector of saggy middles across
all four formats. Two or more consecutive AND-THENs = a flagged sag, name the
beats.

## Pass 3: Open-loop ledger

List every loop the piece opens - a question raised, a promise made, tension
planted, a "here's how I'll do it" - and where each closes.

- **Unclosed loop** = broken trust with the reader. Flag it.
- **Loop closed too early** = the retention cliff; nothing left to stay for.
  Flag the beat where the last loop closes vs. where the piece actually ends.
- The **title/subject/hook loop** is special: it must stay open until the
  climax and must genuinely close (curiosity with payoff, not bait).

Grounded in information-gap theory: curiosity is the gap between what the
reader was made to want to know and what they know. No gap, no pull.

## Pass 4: Contrast and hero audit

- **Contrast (Duarte sparkline):** persuasive pieces oscillate between "what
  is" and "what could be," ending in the new normal. A flat line - all
  present-state complaint, or all vision - moves no one. Check the piece
  alternates.
- **Hero check (StoryBrand):** the reader/viewer is the hero; the writer (and
  any sponsor/brand) is the guide. Flag every stretch where the writer or the
  sponsor becomes the hero of their own story - in sponsored content this is
  the most common structural failure and the fastest credibility leak.

## Pass 5: Argument audit (Toulmin per load-bearing claim)

Identify the load-bearing claims - the ones the piece falls apart without.
For each, extract: **claim / grounds (evidence) / warrant (the silent
assumption connecting them) / qualifier / rebuttal.** Marketing arguments are
almost always enthymemes: the warrant is unstated. The failure modes to hunt:

1. **Missing warrant the audience won't grant.** "Teeth 50% whiter" only
   supports "buy this" if the reader wants whiter teeth. Check every silent
   warrant against the profiled audience, not against a generic reader.
2. **Grounds-free claims.** Assertions with no evidence. Burden of proof is
   proportional to boldness: a category-defying claim needs named proof; a
   table-stakes claim needs none. Don't flag the latter.
3. **Falsifiability (Harry Dry's test).** Can the claim be proven true or
   false? Can you visualize it? Could a competitor say it verbatim?
   "Seamless workflows" fails all three; "I generated 900k impressions from 10
   posts" passes all three.
4. **Missing rebuttal.** The obvious objection the target reader *will* raise
   that the piece never addresses. Steelman the skeptic: write their objection
   in their own words, then check whether the piece survives it.
5. **Promise-payoff mismatch.** Whatever burden the opening takes on (the
   challenge set, the question posed, the number promised), verify the ending
   actually discharges it - not a smaller adjacent payoff.

## Pass 6: Audience fit

Run against the creator profile from Step 1 of the skill, never a generic
reader.

1. **Awareness match (Schwartz's 5 stages).** Classify the intended reader:
   Unaware / Problem-aware / Solution-aware / Product-aware / Most-aware. Then
   check the piece's *entry point* matches. The two commonest mismatches:
   leading with product/features at a problem-aware reader, and re-explaining
   the problem to a product-aware one.
2. **Sophistication match.** How many times has this audience heard this claim
   shape? In a sophisticated market a bigger promise reads as noise - the
   piece must shift to mechanism ("here's *how*") or identity ("this is for
   people like you"). Flag inflated promises aimed at jaded readers.
3. **One-reader test.** Force the piece against a single named (or sketched)
   reader and their job-to-be-done: are they here to learn, feel seen, decide,
   or be entertained? Score each beat: would *this person* keep going here?
   Does the payoff complete *their* job, not the writer's?
4. **Proof-type credibility.** Does the piece use the proof this segment finds
   credible (peer stories vs. data vs. demo vs. named logos)? Structural
   question, not a wording one.
5. **The sponsored-content read.** If a brand paid for or appears in the
   piece, the audience reads it with one extra question: "is this person
   telling me the truth or earning a check?" Every sponsor mention must
   survive that read. Flag any beat where the sponsor gets hero treatment,
   superlatives the creator can't verify, or screen time the story doesn't
   need.

## Principles for the output (what makes a note usable)

1. **What's broken, not how to rewrite it.** A fix may be *illustrated* ("this
   beat needs the number and the date"), never prescribed as prose.
2. **Every note quotes or names its beat.** A note that can't point at the
   draft is a vibe, not a finding.
3. **Rubric over emotion.** Score against the passes above and the format
   checklist; "I didn't love the middle" is banned.
4. **Rank by structural leverage.** Lead with the fix that unblocks the most
   downstream beats. Three notes that move retention beat fifteen
   observations.
5. **No mandate.** Frame as "this beat isn't landing because X," never "you
   must." The creator owns the draft.
6. **Protect the load-bearing beats.** Name what works so the writer knows
   what not to break in revision - that's information, not politeness.
