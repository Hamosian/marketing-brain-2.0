# Customer Profile: jobs, pains, gains

The right-hand side of the Value Proposition Canvas. It describes a customer
segment (or a single B2B stakeholder) as they are, independent of anything
Riverside sells. Condensed from *Value Proposition Design* (Osterwalder,
Pigneur, Bernarda, Smith; Wiley, 2014), chapter 1.1, with Riverside mapping.
The canvas itself is Strategyzer's, licensed CC BY-SA.

## Customer jobs

What the customer is trying to get done, in their words, not ours. Three main
types plus supporting jobs. A profile that lists only functional jobs is
incomplete; social and emotional jobs are often the ones that decide.

| Type | What it is | Riverside examples |
|---|---|---|
| Functional | A task to perform or a problem to solve | Record a remote guest at studio quality; publish a weekly episode; run a 400-person webinar; cut clips for LinkedIn |
| Social | How the customer wants to be perceived | Look like a professional production, not a hobbyist; look like a team of five when it is one person; be the marketer who ships video without an agency |
| Personal / emotional | A feeling state they seek | Peace of mind that the recording will not fail with a paying client on the line; relief from post-production dread |
| Supporting: buyer | Comparing, deciding, purchasing, taking delivery | Work out how many licences a 40-person agency needs; get a price before a call; get a gated feature unlocked before Friday's recording |
| Supporting: co-creator | Reviewing, giving feedback, shaping the product | Posting in the community; beta testing; feature requests |
| Supporting: transferrer | Ending, cancelling, exporting, handing over | Export the archive; move a show to a new owner; cancel a seat |

**Job context** changes the job. Recording alone at midnight, recording a
client webinar, recording in a shared office: same customer, different
constraints, different priorities. Add context notes to the profile when it
matters (when, where, with whom, constraint).

**Job importance** is a ranking, not a list. Some jobs are crucial because
failure has serious consequences or because they recur weekly.

Ask **why** several times. "Learn to edit faster" may really be "publish
twice a week", which may really be "grow the show enough to sell sponsorship".
Stop when you reach the job that actually drives behaviour.

## Customer pains

Anything that annoys the customer before, during, or after a job, or that
stops them starting. Three types:

- **Undesired outcomes, problems, characteristics.** Functional (the recording
  dropped), social (I looked unprepared in front of the client), emotional (I
  dread the edit), ancillary (I have to email guests setup instructions).
- **Obstacles.** What prevents or slows the job: no time, no budget, cannot
  get approval, guests will not install software.
- **Risks.** What could go wrong: a corrupted file after a one-shot interview,
  a security review that fails, a webinar that caps at 100 when 130 registered.

**Pain severity**: extreme to moderate. Rank it.

**Make pains concrete.** "Takes too long" becomes "more than 40 minutes of
post per 30-minute episode". Ask how the customer measures the pain; that
measure is what a pain reliever must move.

### Trigger questions for pains (paraphrased)

1. How does this customer define too costly: time, money, effort?
2. What makes them feel bad: frustrations, annoyances, headaches?
3. Where do current solutions underperform: missing features, performance,
   malfunctions?
4. What are the main difficulties: understanding how things work, getting a
   specific thing done, resistance to a particular job?
5. Which negative social consequences do they fear: loss of face, power,
   trust, status?
6. Which risks do they fear: financial, social, technical, "what could go
   wrong"?
7. What keeps them awake: big issues, concerns, worries?
8. Which common mistakes do they make, including using a solution the wrong
   way?
9. Which barriers stop adoption: upfront cost, learning curve, other
   obstacles?

## Customer gains

The outcomes and benefits the customer wants. Four types, from must-have to
would-never-have-asked-for:

| Type | Definition | Riverside examples |
|---|---|---|
| Required | Without it the solution does not work | The recording exists and is usable |
| Expected | Basic, assumed even if not strictly necessary | Guest joins from a browser link; audio is clean |
| Desired | Beyond expected, would love it, would name it if asked | One click to clips for three platforms; transcript ready when the call ends |
| Unexpected | Beyond what they would think to ask for | A finished episode edited by the time they have made coffee |

**Gain relevance**: essential to nice-to-have. Rank it.

**Make gains concrete.** "Better performance" becomes "cut post-production
from seven hours to two", which is a real power-user verbatim class in
`references/messaging/power-user-interviews.md`.

### Trigger questions for gains (paraphrased)

1. Which savings would make them happy: time, money, effort?
2. What quality levels do they expect, and what would they want more or less
   of?
3. How do current solutions delight them: which features, what performance?
4. What would make their work or life easier: flatter learning curve, more
   service, lower cost of ownership?
5. Which positive social consequences do they want: looking good, more status
   or power?
6. What are they looking for most: design, guarantees, specific features?
7. What do they dream about, aspire to, or would find a big relief?
8. How do they measure success and failure of the job?
9. What would raise their likelihood of adopting: lower cost, less
   investment, lower risk, better quality?

## Ranking and the shape of a good profile

Order each column: most important jobs, most extreme pains, most essential
gains on top. Start with your own ranking if that is all you have, tag it
`Assumed`, and test it until it reflects the customer's ranking.

A good profile is crowded. Customers have many pains and expect many gains.
Six sticky notes means you do not yet understand the segment.

### Common mistakes and the fix

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Several segments in one profile | One canvas per segment; in B2B one per stakeholder |
| Mixing jobs with outcomes | Jobs are tasks, problems, needs; gains and pains are the outcomes they want or want to avoid |
| Functional jobs only | Social and emotional jobs often outrank them ("look professional in front of the client") |
| Listing with the offer in mind | Forget the product. A podcaster chooses between Riverside, Zoom, an editor on Upwork, and not recording at all |
| Too few notes | Go beyond what the proposition addresses; map everything that matters to them |
| Vague pains and gains | Write the number: how long is too long, how much is a raise |

### Pains are not the mirror image of gains

For "earn more from the show", do not write "more revenue" as the gain and
"less revenue" as the pain. Find how much more feels like a gain, then put the
**barriers** ("sponsors want video, I only have audio") and the **risks**
("if the show stalls I lose the sponsor") in pains.

## B2B: one canvas per stakeholder

Riverside's Business deals involve several people whose jobs, pains, and
gains differ. Sketch a profile for each that can tilt the decision:

| Stakeholder | Typical Riverside role | Distinct concern |
|---|---|---|
| End user | Producer, host, video editor, social manager | Reliability, quality, throughput, guest experience |
| Economic buyer | Marketing lead, founder, agency owner | Licence maths, price before a call, budget justification |
| Decision maker | VP Marketing, Head of Content, CEO of a small firm | Fit with the content strategy, risk, vendor consolidation |
| Recommender | The person running the evaluation | Comparison against Zoom, StreamYard, Descript, Goldcast |
| Influencer | Agency partner, a creator they follow, a peer | Credibility, what "professionals" use |
| Saboteur | IT, security, procurement, the owner of the incumbent tool | SSO, data handling, one more vendor, migration effort |

The `.claude/skills/inbound-demo-reply/pain-map.md` cross-cutting finding applies here: many demo requesters
are existing users who hit a plan wall (a gated feature, a webinar cap, a
second seat). Their top "pain" is packaging friction, and the strongest
reliever is removing it. Do not invent a deep pain where the evidence shows a
purchase obstacle.

## Same customer, different contexts and different solutions

Add context rows to a profile when priorities shift by situation. And remember
the customer's alternatives are not only competing recording tools: an
outsourced editor, a Zoom recording plus a freelancer, a lower publishing
cadence, or doing nothing all compete for the same job.

## Identify high-value jobs

Score each job on four questions (0 to 3 each). Focus the proposition on the
highest totals, but check the top jobs against the pain map's won-vs-lost
table before committing.

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Important | Does failing the job cause extreme pain or forfeit an essential gain? |
| Tangible | Can the customer feel the pain or see the gain immediately or often? |
| Unsatisfied | Do current solutions fail to relieve it, or not exist? |
| Lucrative | Do many customers have it, or will a few pay a premium for it? |

## Six ways to innovate from a finished profile

1. Address more jobs, including related and ancillary ones.
2. Switch to a more important job than the one everyone competes on (Hilti
   sold "deliver the project on time", not drills).
3. Go beyond functional jobs to social and emotional ones.
4. Help a lot more customers do a job that was too complex or expensive.
5. Get the job done incrementally better on the dimensions that matter.
6. Get the job done radically better and create a new market.

## Earlyvangelists

When looking for who to interview or pilot with, prefer customers who: have
the problem, know they have it, are actively looking for a solution, have
cobbled together an interim fix, and have or can get budget. In Riverside's
data these look like the demo requesters with a dated recording, a seat
count, and a named incumbent they are leaving.
