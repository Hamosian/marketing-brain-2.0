# Register: internal ask

Nir asking a colleague or a vendor for something. Slack most often, sometimes email. An intro, a name, some of their time, a decision.

**The shape: state the fact, ask the question, stop.** Two short paragraphs at most. Open on what is true ("We're working with X as Y"), then the ask as a plain question. Then nothing.

## The five failures, all of them adding rather than cutting

Every one of these was a real correction on one three-line Slack message, 2026-09-02, four rounds.

1. **Never frame it as a call for volunteers.** "Looking for 1-2 volunteers", "who's in?", "any takers", "if anyone has bandwidth". Nir asks directly. He is a director asking colleagues, not running a raffle.
2. **Never argue why the ask benefits them.** A line like *"he's booking the meetings that land on your calendars, so this comes back to you"* is redundant and it is the clearest pitch tell there is. Colleagues do not need the benefit sold to them. Cut the whole sentence.
3. **Keep Nir's phrasing when he has given it.** If he wrote "so he can get a better understanding into how to pitch the product for different personas and industries", that sentence goes in the message. Do not sharpen it into something tidier. He gave you the words because they are the words he wants.
4. **Never invent scope.** No extra deliverable, no options menu, no rationale he did not give. Adding "plus a couple of recorded calls he can listen back to" invented a second ask nobody wanted.
5. **Four tidy single-topic paragraphs is itself the tell.** Let the thoughts run together. Two paragraphs, commas doing the work of paragraph breaks.

## Ask the logistics question out loud

The thing that actually unblocks the ask usually goes unasked. Name it:

- An intro: who would be a good person to talk to, **and** should he or they make the introduction.
- Time: how much, and by when.
- A decision: what happens if they say nothing.

One question per paragraph maximum. Two question marks in a short message is the ceiling.

## Worked example

The ask, in Nir's own words: *"I want to intro Vince from memoryBlue to 1-2 AEs from the US team so he can get a better understanding into how to pitch the product for different personas and industries."*

**Shipped:**

> Morning! We're working with Vince from memoryBlue as an external SDR agency and he needs to get a better understanding of how to pitch the product for different personas and industries.
>
> Who'd be two good people for him to talk to, so he hears a couple of different styles? And do you want to do the intro here or should I?

Why it works: opens on the fact, carries his phrasing intact, the reason for two people is his own ("different styles"), and both logistics questions are asked out loud. Nothing argues, nothing sells, nothing is invented.

**What it replaced, and why each was wrong:**

> ~~Hey, looking for 1-2 volunteers.~~ → volunteer framing
> ~~He's booking the meetings that land on your calendars, so this comes back to you.~~ → arguing the benefit
> ~~One call each, plus a couple of recordings he can listen back to.~~ → invented scope
> ~~If two of you can take it, one from the sub-1000 side and one from enterprise would cover both ends.~~ → a rationale Nir never gave

## An email runs longer than a Slack ask, but the voice does not change

A vendor email can be more than two paragraphs. The shape still holds inside it: state the fact, ask the question, no selling. What must not change is that it sounds like Nir talking to the person, in full human sentences.

## No ops shorthand in an outward email

The fastest way to sound not-like-Nir is to drop internal list-processing shorthand into a partner-facing message. It reads like a spreadsheet talking. Banned, with the natural version beside it:

- "here's the read on the list" → "here's where we landed" or "I went through it with the team"
- "clear / drop the ones that won't run" → "a few you can take off the list"
- "three live meetings from this batch" → "three of these are ones we'd like to meet"
- "pull more candidates", "same profile:" → "do you have other people you could put in front of us?"
- a bucket header like "Keep scheduling these three:" → a plain sentence that introduces the names

Filed 2026-09-17, after a Boscia partner email came back "this is not my voice." The register was right; the phrasing was internal ops-speak. Nir talks like a person even when the content is a list.

## Mechanics

- Greeting: "Hey," or "Morning!" both fine. Match the hour.
- Lowercase and run-on commas are fine in Slack. Do not tidy them out.
- No bold unless exactly one thing cannot be missed. No headers. No bullets unless listing real items.
- No neat closer. End on the question. Cutting the last sentence usually improves it.
- Slack channel over DM when the ask could be answered by any of several people, so the answer is visible to all of them. The `slack-agent` conventions still apply on top of this.
