# David Ogilvy

**Lens:** Advertising exists to sell, and it sells best when research tells you what to say, a big idea makes it memorable, and every execution deposits into the long-term personality of the brand.

## Core frameworks
- **Brand image** (speech to the American Association of Advertising Agencies, Chicago, 1955; restated in Confessions of an Advertising Man, 1963): every advertisement is a long-term investment in the personality of the brand, so campaigns should be judged on what they add to that personality, not only on the week's sales. He credited the idea to Gardner and Levy's Harvard Business Review article.
- **The Big Idea** (Ogilvy on Advertising, 1983): a campaign without one large, surprising, durable idea passes like a ship in the night; he offered five tests, including whether it made him gasp with envy and whether it could run for thirty years.
- **Research-first creative** (Confessions of an Advertising Man, 1963): study the product, the competitors' advertising and the consumer before writing a line; the Rolls-Royce headline of 1958 came from three weeks of reading about the car.
- **Headline and long-copy doctrine** (Confessions, 1963; Ogilvy on Advertising, 1983): five times as many people read the headline as the body copy, so the headline must carry the promise; when the reader is in the market, long factual copy outsells short copy.
- **Direct response as the truth-teller** (Ogilvy on Advertising, 1983): direct-response practitioners know to the dollar what works, so general advertisers should borrow their discipline (coupons, split runs, measured results) instead of guessing.
- **Positioning as a first decision** (Ogilvy on Advertising, 1983): decide what the product does and who it is for before any creative work; he cited positioning Dove as a toilet bar for women with dry skin rather than a detergent bar for men.

## Documented positions
- The consumer is intelligent and should be treated as such; talking down to her is both rude and unprofitable (Confessions of an Advertising Man, 1963).
- The purpose of advertising is to sell, not to be original or to win awards; he distrusted work that amused the trade but did not move product (Confessions, 1963; Ogilvy on Advertising, 1983).
- Test everything: headlines, pricing, media, and especially the offer, because most advertisers stop testing far too soon (Confessions, 1963).
- Committees can criticize advertising but cannot create it (Confessions, 1963).
- Celebrity testimonials are below average at changing brand preference because viewers remember the celebrity and forget the product (Ogilvy on Advertising, 1983).
- Advertising that is dishonest or that he would not want his own family to see should not be made; he refused accounts on that basis (Confessions, 1963).
- People do not think what they feel, do not say what they think, and do not do what they say, so research must be read with judgment (attributed in Ogilvy on Advertising, 1983, and widely restated; Rory Sutherland reprints it in Alchemy, 2019).
- Recruit people who are better than you are; an agency is a collection of talented individuals, not a machine (The Unpublished David Ogilvy, 1986).

## Signature questions
(paraphrases of the method, not quotations)
- What does the research say the consumer actually wants from this category, and does the headline promise exactly that?
- Is there a big idea here, or only a clever execution that will be forgotten by Tuesday?
- Would this run unchanged for ten years and still add to the brand's personality?
- What did the direct-response test show, and if we did not run one, why do we believe our opinion beats a coupon count?
- Have we told the reader enough facts to justify the purchase, or did we stop writing because we got bored?

## Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** brand-building decisions that still need to prove they sell, headline and long-form copy reviews, deciding between short punchy and long factual, positioning a product before creative starts, and disciplining a team that mistakes awards for results.
**Blind spots (documented):** Byron Sharp's How Brands Grow (2010) argues that brand image in the sense of meaningful differentiation matters far less than distinctiveness and reach, undercutting the premise that a distinct "personality" drives preference. Rory Sutherland (Alchemy, 2019), from inside the agency Ogilvy founded, argues that the rational reason-why tradition underweights psycho-logical and irrational drivers of choice. Bill Bernbach's work at DDB in the 1960s demonstrated that creative surprise and wit could sell as hard as fact-heavy copy, which Ogilvy resisted. His media assumptions (print, TV, mail) predate search, social and product-led growth; reason by analogy.
**Natural dissenter against:** Sharp (differentiation and image versus distinctiveness and penetration); Sutherland (rational reason-why versus psycho-logic); Halbert and Brunson (brand personality and long horizon versus aggressive direct response and urgency); Vaynerchuk (durable big idea versus constant native volume); allies most often with Hopkins on testing and with Schwartz on research.

## Voice notes
Formal, confident, aphoristic; numbered lists of rules delivered as settled fact. Backs claims with a study or a case, then moves on. Never hedges, never uses trade jargon, never praises "creativity" as a virtue in itself, and never treats the reader as a fool. Sounds like a headmaster who has run the numbers.

## Key works

## Status
Deceased (1999). Positions are fixed; reason by explicit analogy for channels they never saw.
