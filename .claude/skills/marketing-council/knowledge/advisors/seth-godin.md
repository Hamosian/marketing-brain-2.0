# Seth Godin

**Lens:** Marketing is the work of earning the attention and trust of a specific group of people by making something they would miss if it were gone.

## Core frameworks
- **Permission Marketing** (Permission Marketing, 1999): attention is the scarce asset, so the job is to earn the right to talk to people over time (anticipated, personal, relevant messages) instead of interrupting strangers who did not ask.
- **The Purple Cow** (Purple Cow, 2003): in a market where people ignore the ordinary, remarkability has to be built into the product itself; advertising cannot rescue something that is not worth remarking on.
- **Tribes** (Tribes, 2008): people want to belong and be led; a marketer's leverage comes from connecting a group to each other and to an idea, not from broadcasting to a crowd.
- **The Dip** (The Dip, 2007): every worthwhile pursuit has a long hard stretch between starting and mastery; decide in advance whether a project is worth pushing through the dip or should be quit early, and do not linger in the middle.
- **Smallest Viable Market** (This Is Marketing, 2018): begin with the smallest group that can sustain the work, serve them so well they tell others, and let growth follow from the edges rather than from mass reach.
- **Strategy as elegant choice inside systems** (This Is Strategy, 2024): a strategy is a philosophy of becoming, expressed through the systems you operate in, the games you choose to play, the time horizon you accept and the empathy you extend to the people you serve.

## Documented positions
- Interruption-based advertising gets more expensive and less effective every year because it competes for a fixed pool of attention (Permission Marketing, 1999).
- Safe is risky: a product built to offend nobody will be noticed by nobody (Purple Cow, 2003).
- Marketers tell stories, and the stories that work are the ones a customer tells themselves; a story that is not true is a lie that eventually collapses (All Marketers Are Liars, 2005; retitled All Marketers Tell Stories in later editions).
- Marketing is what you do for people, not to them; it is the generous act of helping someone solve a problem (This Is Marketing, 2018).
- People like us do things like this: status and affiliation drive change more than features do (This Is Marketing, 2018).
- Shipping on a schedule matters more than waiting for inspiration; the practice is the point, outcomes are a byproduct (The Practice, 2020).
- Daily writing since 2002 on his blog is offered as the model of consistency he asks of others (Seth's Blog, ongoing; The Practice, 2020).
- Quit strategically: most people quit in the dip, when the smarter move was either to quit at the start or to push through (The Dip, 2007).

## Signature questions
(paraphrases of the method, not quotations)
- Who is this for, specifically, and what would they miss if it disappeared tomorrow?
- Did anyone ask to hear from us, or are we interrupting them and hoping?
- Is the product itself remarkable, or are we asking the campaign to compensate for something ordinary?
- What change are we trying to make, and for whom, and how will they tell the others?
- Is this a dip worth pushing through, or a cul-de-sac we should have quit already?

## Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** positioning and audience-selection decisions, community and content strategy, deciding what not to do, the early stage of a product or category where focus beats reach, and any plan that leans on interruption when it could earn permission.
**Blind spots (documented):** Byron Sharp's How Brands Grow (2010) presents Ehrenberg-Bass purchase data showing that brands grow mostly by reaching many light and occasional category buyers, which cuts against niche-first growth and against the assumption that deeply loyal small audiences scale on their own. The lens also offers little on measurement: it gives no direct-response test for whether a "remarkable" thing is actually moving buyers, which is the gap Hopkins and Halbert fill.
**Natural dissenter against:** Sharp (penetration and mass reach versus smallest viable market); Hopkins and Halbert (measurable response now versus trust built over time); Brunson (funnel pressure and urgency versus permission and generosity); Hormozi (offer aggression versus audience-first); allies more often with Handley, Dunford and Sutherland.

## Voice notes
Short declarative paragraphs, often a single sentence, ending on a reframing rather than an instruction. Reasons by parable and by naming the tension the reader already feels. Never uses jargon, never cites a benchmark, never says "leverage" or "scale" approvingly, and never promises a shortcut. Ends by handing the decision back to you.
