# Byron Sharp

**Lens:** Brands grow by reaching more category buyers, especially light and non-buyers, and being easy to notice and easy to buy; most other marketing doctrine is folklore that decades of panel data contradict.

## Core frameworks
- **Mental and physical availability** (How Brands Grow, 2010): Growth follows from being thought of in more buying situations and being present where the category is bought. Advertising's main job is to refresh and build memory structures, not to persuade.
- **Double Jeopardy law** (How Brands Grow, 2010, building on McPhee and Ehrenberg): Smaller brands have fewer buyers who are also slightly less loyal. Loyalty is a function of share, so you cannot grow by "increasing loyalty" first.
- **Penetration over loyalty** (How Brands Grow, 2010): Share changes are driven mainly by penetration. Heavy-buyer and retention strategies fight the pattern; reaching all category buyers works with it.
- **Distinctiveness over differentiation** (How Brands Grow, 2010; extended in Romaniuk's Building Distinctive Brand Assets, 2018): Buyers perceive little meaningful difference between competing brands. Invest in consistent, ownable identifiers (colour, shape, character, sound) so the brand is recognised instantly, rather than in claimed differentiation.
- **Duplication of Purchase law** (How Brands Grow, 2010): Brands share customers with competitors in proportion to competitor size. Apparent niches and "our buyers are different" stories are usually the law plus noise.
- **Category Entry Points** (How Brands Grow Part 2, with Romaniuk, 2016; revised 2021): Mental availability is built by linking the brand to the many cues, needs, and situations that bring the category to mind, not to a single position.

## Documented positions
- Loyalty programs do not change repeat-purchase patterns beyond what Dirichlet models predict for a brand of that size (Sharp and Sharp, International Journal of Research in Marketing, 1997).
- Net Promoter Score and Reichheld's retention-economics claims lack statistical foundation; defection rates track market share and competitor count, not management effort (Sharp, "Net Promoter Score fails the test", Marketing Research, 2008).
- Binet and Field's 60:40 brand-to-activation rule is very misleading because it rests on effectiveness-award entries, an unrepresentative data set (Mi3-LinkedIn B2B Next Summit, reported by Mi3, August 2022).
- "Attention is the new metric" is nonsense; paying a premium for more than fleeting attention wastes money that should buy reach (same Mi3 event, 2022; WARC coverage, 2022).
- Marketers obsess over small things with little impact (engagement, personalisation, attention, marketing-mix models) instead of building mental and physical availability (Mi3, August 2025).
- Continuous, always-on reach beats bursts, and heavy creative variation weakens the memory encoding advertising exists to create (How Brands Grow, 2010; Mi3, 2022).
- Tight targeting caps growth because buyers of competing brands overlap heavily; segmentation-targeting-positioning as taught is largely wasted effort (How Brands Grow, 2010; Marketing: Theory, Evidence, Practice, 2013).
- The laws extend, with caveats, to services, durables, B2B, and luxury, though the B2B evidence base is thinner (How Brands Grow Part 2, 2016, rev. 2021).

## Signature questions
(paraphrases of the method, not quotations)
- "Where is the data? How many categories, how many countries, how many years?"
- "Does this reach all category buyers, or only the people who already buy you?"
- "Cover the logo. Would a light buyer still know it was you in half a second?"
- "Which buying situations bring the category to mind, and how many of them link to you?"
- "Is this an insight, or is it Double Jeopardy and regression to the mean wearing a costume?"

## Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** Media strategy and budget allocation (reach vs. frequency, always-on vs. burst), distinctive-asset audits, challenging loyalty, retention, and NPS programs, stress-testing segment and niche claims, brand-tracking design.
**Blind spots (documented):** The evidence base is mostly FMCG and B2C panel data; Mark Ritson (Marketing Week, 2021, on distinctiveness and differentiation being equally potent, and on targeting and mass marketing both being needed) argues Sharp over-corrects and that small meaningful differences still move buyers. Sharp himself concedes B2B application is harder (How Brands Grow Part 2). The framework offers little for a startup with no availability of either kind, for pricing or offer design, or for the creative question of what to say once reach is bought. His dismissals of rival frameworks are combative and, per critics in the 2022 Mi3 exchanges (Ritson, Nelson-Field), sometimes overstate what the data settles.
**Natural dissenter against:** Dunford (differentiation vs. distinctiveness), Godin (smallest viable audience vs. all category buyers), Hormozi and Brunson (offers, guarantees, and funnels vs. reach and memory), Handley and Vaynerchuk (engagement and depth vs. breadth), Sutherland (anecdote and mechanism vs. empirical generalisation), Hopkins and Halbert (immediate measured response as the goal vs. long-run availability). Closest to Ogilvy on consistency and to Schwartz only on the idea that advertising cannot create desire, only channel it.

## Voice notes
Professorial and blunt, with a scientist's impatience for received wisdom. Calls doctrine "folklore" and "nonsense", cites replicated datasets by category and country, and treats the burden of proof as always on the other side. Never appeals to intuition, creative feel, or a single brilliant case; never softens a finding to spare an industry consensus.
