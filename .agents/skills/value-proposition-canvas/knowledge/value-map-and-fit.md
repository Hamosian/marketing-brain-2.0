# Value Map, Fit, and assessing the proposition

The left-hand side of the canvas (what we design and control), the Fit check
that joins it to the Customer Profile, and the tools for judging and choosing
between alternative propositions. Condensed from *Value Proposition Design*
chapters 1.2, 1.3, 2.1, and 2.4, with Riverside mapping.

## Products and services

A plain list of what the proposition is built on for **this** segment: the
shop window. Include supporting items that help the buyer compare and buy,
the co-creator contribute, and the transferrer leave. Types: physical,
intangible (support, guarantees), digital (the product, templates,
integrations), financial (plans, trials, credits).

Rank by relevance to the customer. Not everything on the pricing page is
essential to every segment.

Products and services create no value on their own. Value exists only in
relation to a specific segment's jobs, pains, and gains.

## Pain relievers

How, exactly, the products and services alleviate specific pains. Each
reliever names the pain it kills. A proposition does not need a reliever for
every pain; the best ones relieve a few extreme pains extremely well.

Trigger questions (paraphrased). Could the products and services:

1. Produce savings in time, money, or effort?
2. Make the customer feel better by removing frustrations and headaches?
3. Fix underperforming solutions with new features, better performance,
   higher quality?
4. End specific difficulties by making things easier or removing obstacles?
5. Wipe out negative social consequences: loss of face, power, trust, status?
6. Eliminate feared risks: financial, social, technical?
7. Help the customer sleep at night by removing a significant worry?
8. Limit common mistakes, or help use a solution the right way?
9. Remove adoption barriers: upfront cost, learning curve, migration effort?

Rank relievers: essential (relieves an extreme pain, often radically) versus
nice-to-have (relieves a moderate one).

## Gain creators

How the products and services produce the outcomes the customer requires,
expects, desires, or would be surprised by. Same discipline: name the gain
each creator addresses; do not try to create every gain.

Trigger questions (paraphrased). Could the products and services:

1. Create savings that please the customer?
2. Produce outcomes they expect or that exceed expectations in quality, more
   of something, less of something?
3. Outperform current solutions on a specific feature, performance, or
   quality?
4. Make work or life easier through usability, accessibility, more service,
   lower cost of ownership?
5. Create positive social consequences: look good, gain status?
6. Do the specific thing they are looking for: design, guarantees, a feature?
7. Fulfil an aspiration or relieve a hardship they dream about?
8. Produce outcomes that match how they measure success and failure?
9. Make adoption easier through lower cost, less investment, lower risk,
   better quality or design?

### Relievers versus creators, and versus the profile

Relievers address pains, creators address gains, and one item may do both.
The point of both boxes is to make value creation explicit. They are
**different from pains and gains**: you decide the relievers and creators;
you do not decide the customer's pains and gains.

### Common mistakes and the fix

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Listing all products rather than the bundle for this segment | Only what jointly forms the proposition for this segment |
| Putting products in the reliever or creator boxes | Relievers and creators are explanations ("no guest download", "separate tracks"), not product names |
| Relievers and creators that match nothing in the profile | Either the profile is missing a real pain, or the item creates no value |
| Trying to address every pain and gain | Great propositions make choices about what to forgo |

## Fit

Fit is when the map meets the profile: the products and services produce
relievers and creators that match jobs, pains, and gains the customer cares
about. Customers are the judge, jury, and executioner of it.

**Check your fit.** Take each reliever and creator in turn and tick the job,
pain, or gain it addresses. Unticked items are probably not creating value.
Unticked pains and gains are fine; no proposition satisfies them all. The
question is how well the proposition fits what matters most.

### Three kinds of fit

| Stage | Where | Evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| Problem-solution fit | On paper | Evidence customers care about specific jobs, pains, gains; a proposition designed for them. Not yet evidence they care about the proposition |
| Product-market fit | In the market | Evidence the products, relievers, and creators actually create value and get traction |
| Business model fit | In the bank | Evidence the proposition sits in a profitable, scalable model: channels reach them, revenue exceeds cost |

### Multiple fits

Some models need fit with two segments at once. An **intermediary** model
needs a proposition to the reseller as well as the end customer (Riverside
selling through an agency needs the agency to want it too). A **platform**
needs one per side.

## Prototyping alternatives before refining one

Do not fall in love with the first idea. Quickly sketch three to five
directions, then compare.

**Ad-lib** (fill the blanks; write several):

> Our `[products and services]` help(s) `[customer segment]` who want to
> `[job to be done]` by `[verb: reducing, avoiding]` `[pain]` and
> `[verb: increasing, enabling]` `[gain]`. (Unlike `[competing proposition]`.)

**Napkin sketch rules**: one core idea per sketch; what it is, not how it
works; graspable at a glance; pitchable in 10 to 30 seconds.

**Prototyping principles, condensed**: make it visual and tangible; keep a
beginner's mind; create alternatives before refining; stay comfortable in the
liquid state; start low fidelity; expose early and seek criticism; fail early
and cheaply; use creativity techniques; make a deliberately outrageous
version to spark debate; track every alternative and learning.

**Starting points** are not only the customer. New propositions can start
from an existing asset (Riverside's editing stack, the community), a trend
(AI editing), a competitor's move (Descript and Squadcast consolidation), a
new segment, a new partnership, a cost-structure change, or an imported model
from another sector. They must all **end** at jobs, pains, and gains the
customer cares about.

## Ten characteristics of a great value proposition

Use as a 0 to 10 score per line with a one-line reason. Total out of 100; name
the two weakest.

1. Embedded in a great business model.
2. Focuses on the jobs, pains, and gains that matter most.
3. Focuses on unsatisfied jobs, unresolved pains, unrealised gains.
4. Targets few jobs, pains, and gains, but does so extremely well.
5. Goes beyond functional jobs to emotional and social ones.
6. Aligns with how customers measure success.
7. Focuses on jobs, pains, and gains many people have, or that a few will pay
   a lot for.
8. Differentiates from competition on things customers care about.
9. Outperforms competition substantially on at least one dimension.
10. Is difficult to copy.

## Compare against competitors: the Strategy Canvas

From *Blue Ocean Strategy* (Kim and Mauborgne), applied to the Value Map.

1. Pick the proposition to compare.
2. Put the pain relievers and gain creators you want to compare on the x-axis
   as factors of competition (add pains and gains if they describe the
   factors better).
3. Score your proposition 0 to 10 on each factor.
4. Add competing propositions, including ones outside the category (an
   outsourced editor competes with an editing feature).
5. Score them.
6. Read the curves: where are you differentiated, where are you merely level?
   The factors should align with the top jobs, pains, and gains in the
   profile; if they do not, the map was built without the profile.

Riverside's named competitors in buyer language (per `.claude/skills/inbound-demo-reply/pain-map.md`): Zoom for
webinars and quality, StreamYard, Squadcast and Descript, Teams plus OBS,
Goldcast for enterprise webinars. Only 15 CRM competitor fields were
populated in six months; competitor evidence lives in form comments and calls.

## Choosing between alternatives

**Define criteria and score prototypes** 0 to 10. Criteria themes: fit with
strategy, fit with customer insights (strong evidence, no good solution
exists, visible pain), competition and environment (differentiation), relation
to the current business model (builds on strengths, disrupts a cash cow),
financials (market size, revenue, margins), implementation (time to market,
team, access to customers, cost to build, risk of resistance). Score, then
take the top one or two to the market to test; the customer is the final
judge.

**Simulate the voice of the customer.** Role-play the stakeholder (the
producer, the IT reviewer, the founder who wants a price) against the
proposition before testing it for real. Pick the person closest to that
buyer to play them: sales, support, the SDR.

**Understand the context.** Zoom out to key trends, market forces, industry
forces, and macro forces; mark each as strengthening or threatening the
proposition.

## Presenting a proposition without cognitive murder

Present customer-centric, not feature-centric: connect each product to the
job it gets done, the pain it kills, the gain it creates. Simple over complex,
tangible over abstract, only what matters, one piece of information at a
time, with a storyline. Build the canvas up note by note as you speak. Show
rough prototypes early and refined ones late.

### Three types of feedback, and the culture around them

| Type | Sounds like | Useful for | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion | "If we added X I believe it would work" | Logical improvement | Pet ideas of the most powerful person win |
| Experience | "When we did X last time we learned..." | Avoiding known mistakes | Different context, different result |
| Market facts | "We interviewed N people and M% struggled with..." | Reducing real uncertainty | Bad or wrong data misleads |

Feedback providers help ideas evolve; customers and market facts decide.
Listening to a leader over the market only postpones failure. Distinguish
"hard to do" from "worth doing". Ask "why not", "what if", "what else".

**De Bono's hats for structured feedback** (condensed): pitch (3 to 15 min);
white hat, clarifying questions only; black hat, one minute writing why it is
a bad idea, then read out; yellow hat, one minute writing why it is good;
green hat, open discussion of how to evolve it; the team evolves the idea.
Black before yellow neutralises the loudest sceptic.

**Dotmocracy**: gallery of options, equal stickers per person, place freely,
count. It surfaces enthusiasm; it is not the decision.
