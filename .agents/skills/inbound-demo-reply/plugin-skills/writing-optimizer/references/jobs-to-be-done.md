# Jobs To Be Done: A Writing & Communication Framework

**Version:** 1.0
**Purpose:** Reference document for specialists applying JTBD to optimize copy, messaging, and communication
**Scope:** Origin, philosophy, methodology, application by content type, and anti-patterns

---

## I. Origin & Intellectual History

### The Genesis: Clayton Christensen's Innovation Theory

Clayton Christensen's original insight emerged from studying why successful companies fail when disrupted. His answer wasn't about better products - it was that companies were optimizing the wrong variables. In *The Innovator's Solution* (2003) and refined in *Competing Against Luck* (2016), he introduced a profound reframing:

**People don't buy products. They hire them to make progress on a job that has arisen in their lives.**

The canonical example is the milkshake story from Competing Against Luck. A fast-food restaurant was struggling to increase milkshake sales. Product teams tested bigger cups, new flavors, and added toppings. Nothing moved the needle. Then researchers observed something unexpected: customers were buying milkshakes at 6 AM in their cars, before commutes to boring jobs.

The job wasn't "have a delicious breakfast." The job was "have something to do during a long, monotonous commute that won't create a mess and won't bore me."

Understanding the job reframed everything. The solution wasn't better flavor - it was thickness (engagement during the drive), holdability, and a sustained experience. That reframe turned the product problem into a behavioral one.

Christensen's insight was revolutionary because it shifted focus from *what the product is* to *what the customer is trying to become or accomplish*.

### Demand-Side Sales: Moesta & Spiek's Evolution

Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek extended JTBD into a sales and messaging methodology called Demand-Side Sales. Their innovation was adding rigor to job discovery through structured interviewing techniques and, critically, introducing **the Four Forces of Progress**: push, pull, anxiety, and habit.

Their work revealed something that Christensen hinted at but didn't systematize: understanding the job is only half the battle. You must also understand *the forces that prevent people from acting* on the job, and how to overcome them through messaging.

A person might desperately need to solve a problem (strong push), but anxiety about switching, inertia of current habits, or uncertainty about the new solution keeps them stuck. Moesta's contribution was showing how to write messages that neutralize these blockers, not just amplify the pull.

### Alan Klement's Push/Pull/Anxiety/Habit Model & Writing Application

Alan Klement synthesized this into the most practical framework for *writers*. His book *When Coffee and Kale Compete* (2019) made JTBD accessible and actionable, particularly for messaging and copywriting.

Klement's crucial contribution: **Show people the struggle first.** Not with cynicism or manipulation, but with clarity and recognition. People don't want solutions; they want relief from struggles.

His model emphasizes that copy should:
- **Open with the push** (acknowledge what's wrong, frustrating, or inadequate)
- **Highlight the pull** (make the new solution attractive, aspirational)
- **Address anxiety** (neutralize fears about switching)
- **Overcome habit** (explain why now is different, why change is worth the friction)

Klement's work is the missing link between Christensen's theory and actual copywriting practice.

### The Milkshake Story: What It Teaches About Motivation

The milkshake story deserves deeper analysis because it contains the entire JTBD philosophy in miniature:

1. **The job is not what you think** - It's not nutrition, taste, or convenience. It's engagement and sustained satisfaction during boredom.
2. **The job is contextual** - The same product serves different jobs at different times. (Evening socializing = different job than morning commute.)
3. **Functional + emotional + social** - The job bundles together a task (something to consume), an emotional outcome (relief from boredom), and a social one (looking presentable).
4. **The solution must match the job's structure** - A thinner, better-tasting milkshake fails because it doesn't address the actual problem.

For writing, the lesson is simple: **If you describe what the product does instead of what the customer is trying to become, you will misfire.**

### JTBD in Messaging & Copywriting: The Bridge

The move from product design into writing happened because a new class of practitioners realized that the same logic applies to *how you talk about products*, not just how you design them.

A feature-focused email ("Our software has AI-powered reporting") describes the product. A job-focused email ("Stop spending Friday mornings manually compiling reports. Get insights in seconds instead.") describes the progress the customer wants.

The insight: **People evaluate copy based on whether it solves a job they recognize as theirs.**

## II. Core JTBD Philosophy Applied to Writing

### The Central Claim

In JTBD writing, the customer is not the buyer of your product. The customer is the buyer of *progress*. Your job as a writer is to:

1. Identify what progress they want to make
2. Acknowledge what makes that progress hard today
3. Show how your solution enables that progress
4. Use language that resonates as true to their actual situation

**The corollary:** If your writing doesn't make the reader think "That's exactly what I'm trying to do," you're writing about the wrong job.

### The Job is a Transformation, Not a Task

A common error: confusing the job with the *task*.

- **Task:** Use software to track hours
- **Job:** Stop wasting Friday morning on timesheet administration and get visibility into where work time actually goes so you can plan projects better

The task is a function. The job is a progress event: the customer wants to move from "blind" to "sighted" about something that matters to them.

Good writing targets the transformation, not the task. It speaks to *why the transformation matters*, not *how to operate the tool*.

### Three Layers of Jobs

Every product serves customers across three simultaneous dimensions:

**Functional Jobs:** "I need to accomplish X task or solve X problem"
- Example: "Track employee hours"
- This is the baseline. If you can't do the functional job, nothing else matters.
- But describing only the functional job is insufficient. Most copy fails here - it stops at describing what the product does.

**Emotional Jobs:** "I want to feel Y way about myself or my situation"
- Example: "Feel confident I'm managing my team well and not missing hidden inefficiencies"
- Emotional jobs dominate buying decisions but are rarely mentioned explicitly in copy
- They connect to identity, safety, competence, autonomy

**Social Jobs:** "I want others to perceive me as Z"
- Example: "Be seen as someone who runs a tight ship, who has visibility and control"
- Social jobs involve status, belonging, reputation
- In B2B, social jobs are often about not being blamed or being seen as competent

**Most purchase decisions are 80% emotional/social, 20% functional.** Yet most copy inverts this ratio. It leads with features (functional) and never addresses the emotional or social payoff.

For writing that converts, you must weave all three layers. Start with the emotion, anchor in function, elevate with social proof or positioning.

### The Struggling Moment: When Jobs Become Urgent

People don't hire solutions when things are stable. They hire solutions when they reach a moment of sufficient pain, opportunity, or change.

Moesta calls this the **struggling moment**. It's the moment when:
- Something broke that used to work
- A new constraint or goal has emerged
- They tried to solve it themselves and failed
- The costs of the current way suddenly became visible

**Good writing opens by recognizing the struggling moment.** Not by waiting until the second paragraph. Not by leading with features.

If your reader isn't currently in a struggling moment, your copy must either:
1. Help them see a struggle they didn't recognize (make visible something hidden)
2. Articulate a goal they didn't know was possible
3. Wait for them to arrive at the struggle on their own

The writers who win are the ones who can feel the struggling moment in the room and address it directly.

### Feature-Focused Writing Always Fails (Eventually)

Here's the truth: feature-focused writing works *short-term* on already-converted audiences (existing customers, people who already know they have the job). It fails long-term on new audiences and skeptical readers.

Why?

Because features answer "What can it do?" People don't care about that. They care about "Can it help me?"

**Feature-focused example:** "Our CRM has a 360-degree customer view with AI-powered predictive scoring and real-time pipeline management."

**Job-focused example:** "Stop losing deals to surprises. Know which customers are at risk of churning before they leave, and which upsells are actually worth pursuing."

The feature version sounds impressive. The job version sounds like relief.

## III. The Four Forces of Progress: Applied to Messaging

Moesta and Spiek's Four Forces framework is the most actionable JTBD tool for writers. It explains why people don't move toward solutions even when they recognize the job.

### The Four Forces Model

```
Push          ← (dissatisfaction with current state)

Pull          → (attraction to new solution)
               vs.
Anxiety       ← (fear of switching / uncertainty about new)

Habit         ← (inertia, gravitational pull of status quo)
```

Movement happens when: **Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit**

### Force 1: Push (Dissatisfaction with the Current State)

Push is the frustration, inadequacy, or pain with how things are now.

**In writing, Push is:**
- Naming the problem clearly
- Making visible something the reader experiences but hasn't articulated
- Creating contrast between where they are and where they could be
- Using emotional language that validates the struggle

**Push examples (opening lines of strong copy):**

- "You're spending 3 hours a week just entering data manually."
- "Every project goes through the same chaos at the end: nobody knows who owns what, and deadlines slip."
- "Your team is good, but you can't tell if they're actually productive or just busy."

**Common push mistake:** Being too subtle. Readers don't want poetic. They want recognition. If you're dancing around the problem, you're not using push effectively.

**Neutralizing weak push:** If your audience doesn't feel the pain acutely, you won't overcome their habit. Better copy makes the problem *impossible to ignore*.

### Force 2: Pull (Attraction to the New Solution)

Pull is the aspiration, curiosity, or allure of what becomes possible with the new solution.

**In writing, Pull is:**
- Showing what progress looks like
- Describing the desired outcome vividly
- Making the new state appealing, even aspirational
- Creating positive emotion about the transformation

**Pull examples:**

- "Imagine: it's 4 PM on Friday and your weekly report is done. No scrambling, no data entry, no guessing."
- "Your team knows what success looks like this sprint, who's responsible for what, and where you stand in real time."
- "You spend less time managing and more time leading."

**Common pull mistake:** Being too abstract ("increased efficiency") instead of concrete ("3 fewer meetings a week, back your calendar"). Use specifics that create mental images.

**Pull and aspiration:** The strongest pull often references identity or status - not just comfort. People want to become better versions of themselves, to operate at a higher level, to be seen as competent.

### Force 3: Anxiety (Fear of Switching)

Anxiety is the risk, uncertainty, or cost of trying something new.

**In writing, Anxiety manifests as:**
- Skepticism: "Is this really going to work?"
- Switching costs: "Will implementing this disrupt my team?"
- Identity threat: "Does adopting this solution mean I was doing it wrong before?"
- Vendor risk: "What if they go out of business or change their pricing?"

**In writing, you address anxiety by:**
- Naming fears explicitly (then neutralizing them)
- Providing social proof (others like you did this and it worked)
- Showing evidence (case studies, testimonials, data)
- Reducing switching costs (easy onboarding, trial periods, money-back guarantees)
- Demonstrating stability and credibility (team, funding, customer roster)

**Anxiety examples (what to address in copy):**

- "You might worry this requires teaching your team new tools." → "Our onboarding takes 2 hours per person. We handle the setup."
- "Will this actually integrate with systems you already use?" → "Native integrations with Salesforce, HubSpot, and 200+ other tools."

**Common anxiety mistake:** Ignoring it entirely. Leaving anxiety unaddressed means people stay with the status quo. The best copy acknowledges the fear and defuses it with specificity.

### Force 4: Habit (Inertia and Status Quo Bias)

Habit is the gravitational pull of "just keep doing what we're doing." It's psychological inertia + switching costs + "the known is safer than the unknown."

**In writing, Habit appears as:**
- Procrastination and delay: "We'll consider it next quarter"
- The sunk cost fallacy: "We already invested in our current way"
- Organizational friction: "This would require too many people to change their workflows"
- Risk aversion: "If it breaks, we're liable"

**In writing, you overcome Habit by:**
- Creating urgency (the cost of inaction is now higher than the cost of switching)
- Making change feel inevitable ("This is where the industry is headed")
- Removing friction from adoption (small, reversible first steps)
- Creating urgency around opportunity, not just pain (limited spots, seasonal windows)

**Habit examples (what strong copy does):**

- Instead of: "Consider our software sometime" → "Open spots are filling up for onboarding in May. After that, we're closed until August."
- Instead of: "You could switch to us whenever" → "Every month you don't fix this is costing you $X in wasted time."

**The timing of Habit:** Habit is overcome not just through copy, but through *sequence*. Early copy can't overcome deep habit; it just creates awareness. Follow-up, case studies, and social proof gradually reduce habit's pull until the reader is ready to act.

### Balancing the Four Forces in Copy

The mistake most writers make: they only push hard on Pull while ignoring Anxiety and Habit.

**Strong copy:**
- Opens by validating the Push (acknowledging the problem)
- Builds the Pull (showing the desired state)
- Directly addresses Anxiety (credibility, risk reduction, proof)
- Creates urgency to overcome Habit (why now, why change)

Example structure for a landing page:
1. **Headline:** Name the push (the problem)
2. **Subheadline:** Show the pull (the outcome)
3. **Opening section:** Validate the struggle (make push visceral)
4. **Value section:** Detail the pull (what becomes possible)
5. **Social proof:** Address anxiety (others have done this)
6. **CTA context:** Create urgency (why now)

## IV. Writing the Job Statement

### The Job Statement Format

The canonical JTBD job statement follows this structure:

```
When [situation/trigger],
I want to [motivation/desired action],
so I can [expected outcome/progress].
```

**Example:**

"When my team misses a deadline last-minute because nobody knows who's responsible for what, I want to see one clear view of who owns each task and their status, so I can catch problems before they derail the timeline."

This is different from:

- **A feature:** "Project management software with task assignments and status tracking"
- **A benefit:** "Better visibility and accountability"

The job statement includes the context (when), the motivation (what you want to do), and the desired outcome (why it matters).

### Functional vs. Emotional vs. Social Job Layers

Every product serves multiple simultaneous jobs. Strong writing addresses them in layers:

**Functional job (the literal task):**
"When I need to track team hours to understand project costs, I want a system that automatically collects time data, so I can accurately bill clients."

**Emotional job (the feeling/identity):**
"When I'm managing a team and feeling out of control about where time actually goes, I want visibility into what everyone's working on, so I can feel confident I know what's happening."

**Social job (the status/perception):**
"When I'm presenting my management approach to executives, I want to show that I run a tight, data-driven operation, so I'm perceived as someone who has control and visibility."

**All three exist simultaneously.** But notice: the emotional and social jobs are what *actually drive the purchase decision*. The functional job is the justification you tell yourself and others.

Copy that addresses only the functional job will underperform. Copy that weaves in emotional and social jobs will resonate.

### How to Discover the Real Job (Not the Surface Job)

The job people *say* they have and the job they *actually* have are often different.

**Surface job:** "We need better project management software."
**Real job:** "I need to feel like I have control when things get chaotic, and I need to look good in front of my boss."

Discovering the real job requires:

**1. Listening for language markers:**
- Frustration words: "Finally," "Stop," "Always have to," "Never," "Tired of"
- Aspiration words: "Could," "Actually," "Would be able to"
- Identity markers: "If I were..." or "I'd be that person who..."

**2. Asking the "Why" chain:**
- "Why do you need better project management?" → "So we meet deadlines."
- "Why do missed deadlines matter?" → "We lose clients' trust and my reputation takes a hit."
- "Why does your reputation matter?" → "Because I'm trying to be promoted to Director and I need to show I can manage complexity."

The third why is closer to the real job.

**3. Observing context:**
- When do people reach for the solution?
- What triggers them to seek it?
- What happens if they don't solve it?
- What other solutions do they currently use?

**4. Interviewing the struggle:**
The Moesta technique: Ask about a specific time they experienced the problem.
- "Tell me about the last time you struggled with [X]."
- "What happened? What did you try?"
- "What changed? Why did you try something different?"

Listen for the emotional language and context, not just the functional facts.

### The Difference Between the Job and the Solution

**This is critical and constantly confused in writing.**

The job is abstract and transferable. The solution is specific to your product.

**Job:** "I want to understand what my team is actually working on without constantly asking them."

**Solutions** (many possible):
- A project management tool
- A Slack integration that auto-updates status
- A weekly standup with a clear format
- A whiteboard in the office
- A shared spreadsheet

Most writers blur this line. They describe the solution when they should be describing the job.

**Bad copy:** "Our CRM software integrates with Slack, syncs with Salesforce, and updates in real-time."
(This describes the solution.)

**Good copy:** "Stop losing track of where deals stand. Know at a glance which opportunities are moving and which are stuck."
(This describes the job.)

Once you've described the job clearly, *then* you can explain how your specific solution delivers it better than alternatives.

## V. JTBD Copywriting Techniques

### Technique 1: Open with the Struggle, Not the Solution

The fatal opening error: "Here's what we built."
The winning opening: "Here's what you're struggling with."

**Example:**

❌ Wrong: "ProjectHub is a web-based tool for managing team tasks with real-time collaboration features and customizable workflows."

✅ Right: "You're smart. Your team is good. But somehow projects still end up in chaos at the end, with people working on the wrong priorities and deadlines slipping. Sound familiar?"

The second version does three things:
1. Validates the reader's competence (you're not to blame for the problem)
2. Names the specific struggle (not "inefficiency" but "chaos at the end")
3. Invites recognition ("Sound familiar?")

This is opening with push. It makes the reader think "Yes, that's exactly what happens to us."

**The principle:** Readers spend the first 3 seconds deciding "Is this about me?" If you describe your product, the answer is "I don't know yet." If you describe their struggle, the answer is "Yes."

### Technique 2: Use the Customer's Language, Not Company Language

This seems obvious but is constantly violated.

**Inside-out (company language):**
- "Leverage AI-powered insights to optimize resource allocation"
- "Increase operational throughput and reduce latency"
- "Enhance cross-functional synergy through integrated workflows"

**Outside-in (customer language):**
- "See where your team's time is actually going"
- "Stop projects from slipping because of missed handoffs"
- "Know what everyone's working on without asking for updates"

The customer doesn't speak in "operational throughput." They speak in "getting projects done" and "knowing what's going on."

**How to find customer language:**

1. Read support tickets and customer interviews. What words do they use?
2. Find a competitor's customer reviews. What complaints do customers mention?
3. Search LinkedIn for people talking about your industry's problems. What language do they use?
4. Listen to sales calls. What objections and questions come up?

Then, use *their* language, not your language, in your copy.

### Technique 3: The Before/After Transformation Structure

This structure is JTBD's most powerful copywriting format.

```
Before: [Current struggle with specific detail]
        ↓
         [One moment of realization or trigger]
        ↓
After:  [New possibility or relief]
```

**Example (landing page section):**

**Before:**
"Your team finishes a sprint and you have no idea if you're actually progressing toward your goals. You ask people how their projects are going and get vague answers. You set deadlines and they slip. You can't tell if your team is productive or just busy."

**The trigger:**
"Then you realize: you're missing visibility."

**After:**
"Imagine instead: Every Friday morning you know exactly what everyone shipped this week, what's coming next, and where blockers are. You can spot problems before they become disasters. You spend less time in status meetings and more time unblocking your team. People know you're paying attention, so they take deadlines seriously."

This structure works because:
- It establishes the push (the struggle is specific and painful)
- It creates the pull (the new state is vivid and attractive)
- It moves the reader from recognition ("Yes, that's me") to aspiration ("I want that")

### Technique 4: Write for the Moment of Maximum Motivation (The Struggling Moment)

Not everyone is ready to change at the same time. Readers exist on a spectrum:

1. **Unaware** of the job (don't know they have the problem)
2. **Aware** of the job (feel the pain but haven't decided to solve it)
3. **Actively searching** for a solution
4. **Evaluating** options (comparing your solution to alternatives)
5. **Ready to buy** (just need confirmation or logistics)

Different copy works at different stages. But **the strongest copy is aimed at stage 3-4: active searchers who are ready to move.**

Why? Because these readers have maximum motivation. They're in the struggling moment. They've decided change is necessary and are looking for a path forward.

Copy for stage 1-2 readers (unaware/aware) needs to emphasize the push ("Here's a problem you might not have considered") and create urgency.

Copy for stage 3-4 readers can assume the push is felt and focus on pull and anxiety reduction ("Here's why our solution is the best fit for your specific situation").

**The implication:** Your copy needs to speak to the right reader at the right stage of their decision journey. A landing page might open for stage 1-2 readers but peak for stage 3-4 readers because that's when they convert.

### Technique 5: Progress Language - Verbs and Words of Transformation

JTBD copy uses specific language patterns that signal movement, change, relief, and empowerment.

**Progress verbs (strong in JTBD copy):**
- Stop (stop wasting, stop guessing, stop losing)
- Start (start moving faster, start seeing clarity)
- Skip (skip the nonsense)
- Shift (shift from reactive to proactive)
- Transform (transform chaos into clarity)
- Move (move from problem to solution)
- Get (get visibility, get control, get your time back)
- Know (know what's actually happening)

**Compare:**
- Weak: "Our software provides enhanced visibility metrics."
- Strong: "Finally know what's actually happening on your projects."

**Relief words (emotional anchors):**
- Finally
- At last
- No more
- Enough
- Relief
- Peace of mind
- Clarity
- Confidence

**Aspiration words (pull anchors):**
- Imagine
- Picture yourself
- You could
- What if
- Become the kind of leader/manager/team

**Comparing solutions:**
- Weak: "Our platform offers more integrations than competitors."
- Strong: "Works with the tools you already use. No rip-and-replace, no retraining."

The pattern: **Use verbs of movement, adjectives of emotion, and specific outcomes instead of abstract benefits.**

### Technique 6: Writing for Different Buyer Stages

Different content serves different moments in the decision journey:

**Stage 1-2 (Unaware → Aware):**
- Content type: Educational, awareness-building
- Goal: Name the problem and make it visible
- Language: "You might not realize..." "Most teams struggle with..."
- Example: Blog post, industry trend article, educational webinar

**Stage 3 (Actively Searching):**
- Content type: Comparison, positioning, educational
- Goal: Show that your solution addresses the struggle
- Language: "When you're looking for...", "You need something that...", "The best solution..."
- Example: Product overview, feature breakdown, case study

**Stage 4 (Evaluating Options):**
- Content type: Proof, comparison, detail-heavy
- Goal: Show why your solution is better/right for them specifically
- Language: "Unlike [alternative]...", "We're the only one who...", "Here's how we compare..."
- Example: Detailed case study, customer testimonial, comparison chart

**Stage 5 (Ready to Buy):**
- Content type: Logistics, reassurance, next steps
- Goal: Remove final barriers and create momentum
- Language: "Here's how onboarding works...", "Your first week includes...", "We're with you every step..."
- Example: Onboarding guide, customer success story, implementation timeline

**The implication for strategy:** You can't use one piece of copy for all stages. You need copy that speaks to the struggling moment at each stage. A product overview won't work for unaware readers. A blog post won't work for active searchers. Match the content to the reader's stage.

## VI. Diagnosis: Common JTBD Failures in Writing

### Failure 1: Feature-First vs. Job-First Writing

**Feature-first (fails with new audiences):**
"Our software includes AI-powered predictive analytics, automated reporting, custom dashboards, and real-time collaboration features."

**Job-first (wins with new audiences):**
"Stop spending Monday mornings compiling reports from five different systems. Get the insights you need in seconds."

Feature-first writing works only on people who already know they want your product. Job-first writing converts people who know they have a problem but haven't yet committed to a solution.

**Diagnosis:** If your opening describes what you built instead of what the reader struggles with, you're feature-first.

### Failure 2: Using Internal/Company Jargon the Customer Doesn't Recognize

**Internal jargon (reader doesn't understand):**
"Leverage our omnichannel orchestration platform to maximize LTV through data-driven personalization."

**Customer language (reader understands immediately):**
"Stop losing customers because they never hear from you until you need something. Show up with relevant offers at the moment they care."

This happens because teams spend all day in their language. They forget that their customers live in a different world.

**Diagnosis:** Does your copy use words your customers would use in a conversation? Or are you using words they use at industry conferences?

### Failure 3: Describing Outputs Instead of Outcomes

**Output (what the product produces):**
"Our software generates 150+ automated reports and integrates with 50+ tools."

**Outcome (what the customer achieves):**
"Your team gets the data they need without asking for it. You can move faster because you're not waiting for information."

Outputs are facts about the product. Outcomes are what the customer's life or business looks like after using it.

**Diagnosis:** Can you remove your product's name from the sentence and does it still describe value? If not, you're describing the product (output) not the customer's transformation (outcome).

### Failure 4: Ignoring the Emotional/Social Job Entirely

**Functional job only:**
"Our CRM tracks customer interactions and automates follow-up."

**Functional + Emotional:**
"Track customer interactions and never drop the ball on a follow-up. Feel confident you're building relationships that matter."

**Functional + Emotional + Social:**
"Track customer interactions without dropping the ball. Your boss will see you as someone who maintains real relationships, not just moves deals forward."

Most B2B copy stops at functional. This leaves 80% of the motivation on the table.

**Diagnosis:** Does your copy mention how the customer will *feel* or how they'll be *perceived*? Or only what they'll be able to do?

### Failure 5: Writing for the Average Customer Instead of the Struggling Moment

**Average customer (generic):**
"Managers need better visibility into their teams."

**Struggling moment (specific):**
"You're two weeks from launch and you have no idea if you're going to hit the deadline. Your team is scattered, communication is breaking down, and you need to know right now if there are problems you can fix before it's too late."

The first is true but unmemorable. The second makes someone in that exact moment sit up and pay attention.

**Diagnosis:** If your copy could apply to any customer in your industry, you're probably writing for the average. If it could only apply to someone in a specific struggle, you're writing for the moment.

### Failure 6: Anxiety Left Unaddressed

**Ignoring anxiety (fails):**
"Switch to our platform and transform your operations."

**Addressing anxiety (converts):**
"You might worry this requires retraining your team and disrupting workflows. We've simplified onboarding to 4 hours per person, and 95% of teams are fully productive in the first week. We also offer a 30-day money-back guarantee - if you don't see results, you're covered."

Most copy ignores the fears that actually prevent people from switching. Better copy names them and defuses them.

**Diagnosis:** Does your copy mention any of the readers' likely objections or fears? Or does it only describe the upside?

## VII. Application by Content Type

### LinkedIn Posts (Thought Leadership & Reach)

**The JTBD structure for LinkedIn:**

The goal: Get stopped in the feed by naming a struggle, then add insight or transformation.

**Opening line (the hook):**
- Name the struggling moment specifically
- Use "you," "most," or "nobody talks about"
- Create contrast between current and possible

Examples:
- "You're shipping projects 3 weeks late and nobody knows why."
- "Most leaders think their team's problem is laziness. It's usually clarity."
- "Nobody wants to sit through another status meeting."

**Middle (the insight or observation):**
- Explain what's really happening beneath the surface
- Use concrete example or data
- Show the emotional reality, not the logical problem

Example:
"The issue isn't your team's dedication. It's that every week, priorities shift because nobody's actually looking at what matters. People work hard on the wrong things. It's frustrating for them and you."

**End (the pull or implication):**
- What becomes possible if you solve this?
- What's the aspirational version?
- Action or thought prompt

Example:
"The moment you get clarity on priorities, everything changes. Your team stops second-guessing and moves with purpose. You stop feeling like things are always in chaos. You go from firefighting to actually leading."

**Example LinkedIn post (JTBD structure):**

"You finish your week and realize your team shipped great work but none of it moved the needle on what actually matters.

It's not because they're unfocused. It's because priorities aren't clear. Everyone's operating on a different understanding of what success looks like.

By Monday, you're asking for status updates again instead of moving forward.

The fix: One clear picture of what you're building this quarter and why it matters. A weekly check-in that takes 15 minutes, not an hour. And everyone knows exactly what done looks like.

When your team knows what matters, everything else becomes clear."

**Why this works:** Opens with a specific struggle (not "communication is bad" but "shipped work didn't move the needle"). Uses emotional honesty ("It's frustrating"). Describes the aspiration (clarity, purpose). Ends with a concrete implication.

### Cold Outreach Emails

**The JTBD structure for cold email:**

The goal: Recognition + specific insight + low-friction next step.

**Opening (recognition of the job):**
- Acknowledge a specific challenge they likely face
- Make it clear you're talking to *them*, not a segment
- Use their language, not your product language

**Body (insight + relevance):**
- Show you understand the context
- Offer a specific insight or observation about their situation
- Connect it to a broader principle (not to your product)

**Close (pull + minimal ask):**
- What's the opportunity if this problem is solved?
- What's the minimal next step? (not "let's hop on a call," but "quick question" or "worth 10 minutes?")

**Example cold email (bad - feature focused):**

Subject: CRM Tool for Better Customer Management

Hi Sarah,

We have a new CRM tool that helps teams track customer interactions and automate follow-ups. It integrates with your existing systems and has powerful reporting.

Would you be open to a demo?

Best,
Marcus

---

**Example cold email (JTBD):**

Subject: Quick thought on [Company] and sales ops

Hi Sarah,

I was looking at [Company]'s growth trajectory and noticed something: you've scaled from $20M to $100M ARR in three years, which is impressive. But there's usually a moment around that scale where CRM implementations become a bottleneck - not because the tool is bad, but because you've got competing data sources and no single source of truth.

I work with scaling B2B companies on exactly this moment. I helped [similar company] move from five CRMs to one, and it freed up 25 hours a week across sales ops that they redirected to pipeline management.

Might be relevant now or in six months.

Worth 10 minutes to chat about whether this is even on your radar?

Sarah

---

**Why the second works:** It acknowledges a *specific* context (their scale), shows understanding of a *likely* struggle (multiple data sources), provides *concrete evidence* (hours freed up), and makes a *minimal ask* (10 minutes, not a demo).

### Landing Pages

**The JTBD structure for landing pages:**

A landing page has more space, so it can take people through a fuller journey.

**Hero section (push + pull):**
- Headline: Name the struggle (push)
- Subheadline: Show the desired state (pull)

**Problem section (validate the push):**
- Go deeper into the struggle
- Use specific, painful language
- Make the reader think "Yes, that's exactly my situation"

**Solution section (show the pull):**
- Describe what becomes possible
- Be concrete: "You'll spend 3 fewer hours a week on X"
- Use aspirational language: "You can focus on Y instead"

**How it works section (address anxiety):**
- Show the mechanism briefly
- Remove fear about complexity
- "It works because..." (not "Here's how to use it")

**Social proof section (reduce anxiety):**
- Testimonials from similar companies
- Case study with specific results
- "Others like you have tried this and seen Y result"

**Urgency/CTA section (overcome habit):**
- Create a reason to move now
- Limited spots, seasonal window, upcoming deadline
- Low-friction next step (not "Buy now" but "See how it works" or "Let's talk")

### Product Announcements

**The JTBD structure for product announcements:**

Most product announcements lead with "We're excited to announce..." This is about the *company*, not the *customer*.

- Not "We're launching" but "Now you can"

- What is now in reach?

## VIII. Interaction with Other Frameworks

### JTBD + StoryBrand

**Similarities:** Both emphasize customer transformation over product features. Both start by acknowledging the customer's desire or struggle.

**Difference:**
- **StoryBrand** is narrative-driven; it positions your company as the guide and the customer as the hero. It's about telling a *story*.
- **JTBD** is linguistically precise; it's about discovering and articulating the exact job the customer is trying to accomplish. It's about understanding *motivation*.

**In practice:** A StoryBrand narrative often starts with the customer's external problem (the plot). JTBD goes deeper to the *internal* motivation - why that problem matters to this person right now.

You can use both: StoryBrand gives you the narrative structure; JTBD gives you the precise language about what the customer actually wants.

### JTBD + Made to Stick

**Similarities:** Both emphasize concrete language over abstract. Both want memorable, resonant messaging.

**Difference:**
- **Made to Stick** (Chip & Dan Heath) focuses on *memorability*-creating messages that stick in people's minds through simplicity, unexpectedness, credibility, emotion, stories, and statistics (SUCCESs).
- **JTBD** focuses on *relevance*-creating messages that land because they describe a job the reader recognizes as theirs.

**In practice:** JTBD identifies the job; Made to Stick gives you the framework to make *that job description* memorable.

Example:
- JTBD tells you: "The job is 'I want clarity about what my team is actually working on without constant updates.'"
- Made to Stick tells you: "Make that memorable by: opening with the contrast (chaos vs. clarity), using concrete language (not 'improved visibility' but 'know in two minutes what you'd otherwise spend an hour figuring out'), and adding a story that proves the point."

### JTBD + Smart Brevity (and Modern Copywriting Trends)

**Similarities:** All three emphasize directness and getting to the point.

**Difference:**
- **Smart Brevity** is about *structure*-using clear hierarchies and short sentences to be scannable.
- **JTBD** is about *relevance*-using the right language to describe the actual job.

**In practice:** Use JTBD to identify what to say; use Smart Brevity to say it in the clearest structure.

Example:
- JTBD: "What job are we describing?" → "I want to see project status without asking people for updates."
- Smart Brevity: "How do we structure that for scanning?" → "Stop asking for status updates. See project progress in seconds."

### JTBD + Analogical Framing

**Similarities:** Both acknowledge that context shapes how people interpret information.

**Difference:**
- **Analogical Framing** (Noy's framework, for Israeli marketing) is about *positioning*-understanding how to position ideas within a cultural/linguistic context.
- **JTBD** is about *motivation*-understanding what the customer actually wants to achieve.

**In practice:** Use JTBD to identify the job (the motivation); use Analogical Framing to position it in a way that resonates with a specific audience's mental models and cultural references.

## IX. Anti-Patterns and Failure Modes

### Anti-Pattern 1: Over-Psychologizing the Job

**Mistake:** Trying to make every JTBD statement about deep emotional or psychological needs.

"People hire coffee to feel like they're participating in a modern, cosmopolitan culture that values sophistication."

This is real but vague. It's over-indexed on the emotional job and loses the specificity.

**Better:** "People hire coffee at 7 AM in their car during their commute because they want something to do during a boring drive that won't make a mess and will keep them alert."

This is concrete, observable, and doesn't require heavy psychological interpretation.

**The balance:** Look for emotional and social jobs, but keep them grounded in *observable behavior*, not abstract psychology.

### Anti-Pattern 2: Struggle Fatigue (Making Every Sentence About the Problem)

**Mistake:** Opening with struggle, then continuing to hammer on struggle repeatedly, creating fatigue and hopelessness.

"You're losing deals. Your process is broken. Your team doesn't coordinate. Your CRM is disconnected. You don't have visibility. Everything is a mess."

This is painful, not motivating. After a few sentences of this, readers check out.

**Better:** Open with struggle (one paragraph), validate it (one paragraph), then shift immediately to pull and what becomes possible.

"You know this problem. Your team is great but they're scattered. The moment you have clear visibility into what's happening, everything changes.

[Then describe what becomes possible.]"

**The balance:** Struggle is the opening. But spend the majority of the copy describing the pull and what's newly possible.

### Anti-Pattern 3: Misidentifying the Real Job (Confusing Symptoms with Jobs)

**Mistake:** Identifying a surface symptom instead of the underlying job.

**Surface symptom:** "Teams need better project management tools."

**Real job (what they're actually trying to do):** "I need to feel confident that we're going to hit our deadline and that I'll look good in front of my boss."

The second is the job. The first is just a symptom. If you write to the symptom, you miss the motivation.

**How to avoid it:** Go one level deeper with the "Why" chain.
- Why do they need better PM tools?
- Because they miss deadlines.
- Why do missed deadlines matter?
- Because I look bad in front of my boss.

The last answer is closer to the real job.

### Anti-Pattern 4: Writing to the Job So Abstractly That the Product Disappears

**Mistake:** Focusing so much on the job that you never connect it to the actual solution.

"You want to feel in control and know what's happening. You want clarity and confidence."

This is job-focused. But it doesn't tell the reader *how* or *why* your product delivers it. The product and the specific mechanism become invisible.

**Better:** Job + specific connection.

"You want to feel in control and know what's happening. Our platform pulls all your data into one view so you can see your team's progress, blockers, and risks in seconds. That's where the clarity and confidence come from."

**The balance:** Identify the job clearly, then connect it specifically to how your solution delivers it.

### Anti-Pattern 5: Forgetting That the Job Is Contextual

**Mistake:** Writing as if there's one job that all customers have, when the same customer has *different jobs* in different contexts.

"People hire a coffee shop to get coffee."

Actually, someone might:
- Hire coffee at 7 AM to have something to do during their commute (engagement, alertness)
- Hire coffee at 10 AM in a coffee shop to have a place to work quietly (refuge, productivity)
- Hire coffee at 3 PM with friends to have an excuse to spend time together (socialization)

Same product. Different jobs.

**Better:** Recognize that your customer has *multiple jobs* and write to the most urgent one for your specific audience segment.

**The implication:** Different messaging for different contexts/use cases. A landing page for remote workers has a different job than a landing page for office workers, even though the product is the same.

## XI. Quick Reference: JTBD Copywriting Checklist

Before shipping any copy, ask:

- [ ] **Have I named the specific struggle** the customer feels, or am I being generic about a category-level problem?
- [ ] **Have I used the customer's language**, or am I using company/internal jargon?
- [ ] **Does the opening recognize the struggling moment** they're in, or does it describe my product?
- [ ] **Have I addressed the push** (what's wrong with the current way) before diving into the pull?
- [ ] **Have I made the pull visceral and concrete** (not "improve efficiency" but "get three hours back every Friday")?
- [ ] **Have I acknowledged anxiety** or fears about switching, or does the copy ignore them?
- [ ] **Have I addressed habit** (why now, why change) or just described how good the new way is?
- [ ] **Have I described outcomes**, not outputs? (What the customer can do/feel, not what the product does)
- [ ] **Have I included the emotional and social job**, or only the functional one?
- [ ] **Can a smart person who's not in my target market** understand and use my copy? (If yes, I've been too vague.)

| Framework | Primary Use | Key Output |

| **The Four Forces** | Diagnose why people aren't moving toward solution | Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit (message must tip this balance) |
