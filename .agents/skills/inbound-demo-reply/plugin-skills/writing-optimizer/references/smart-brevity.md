## Origin & Philosophy

### Why VandeHei, Allen, and Schwartz Developed Smart Brevity

The three founders faced a crisis that every modern communicator confronts: **reader attention is fragmenting and collapsing.** People don't read anymore - they skim. They don't sit for long-form exposition - they demand immediate value.

When Jim VandeHei created Axios as a media startup, the core insight was radical: **treat your reader's time as more valuable than your own words.** This wasn't a stylistic choice - it was a respect choice. The attention economy had permanently shifted the rules. No amount of quality writing matters if nobody reads it.

Before Axios, both Politico and traditional news outlets operated on broadcast assumptions: editors decided what was important, and readers consumed accordingly. Smart Brevity inverted this relationship. In a world where readers control the remote (via scroll, click, swipe), you don't get to decide what they pay attention to. You have to earn it.

### The Attention Economy Crisis

Research shows that in 2015, the average human attention span dropped to 8 seconds - down from 12 seconds in the year 2000. Simultaneously:

- 79% of web users scan rather than read word-for-word (Nielsen Norman Group)
- Readers spend an average of 37-50 seconds on a typical web page
- Email open rates correlate directly with subject line clarity and speed
- Decision fatigue in communication: the more words, the fewer readers complete the message

The core crisis: **Traditional writing teaches us to explain before we conclude. Smart Brevity flips this. It says: put the conclusion first, then explain only what's necessary.**

### Axioms Born From Journalism

Smart Brevity emerged from 20+ years of newsroom discipline:

1. **The inverted pyramid** (journalism's 150-year-old framework): Most important facts first. Supporting details in descending order of importance.
2. **The five W's** (who, what, when, where, why): Answerable in the first sentence.
3. **Newsroom editing wisdom**: "If your story can be cut from the bottom, it's not important enough to keep."
4. **Respect for reader time**: Journalists learned this from declining print readership. Readers would abandon a story if it didn't grab them immediately.

The insight: **Axios built an entire news organization - and now a software company - around the principle that writing isn't about author preference. It's about reader efficiency.**

## The Core Principles

### 1. One Idea Per Communication

This is the single hardest principle to implement because writers naturally want to be comprehensive. But the research is unambiguous:

**When you present multiple ideas in a single communication, retention of all ideas drops dramatically.** People can't hold multiple complex concepts in working memory simultaneously. They pick one, discard the others, or disengage entirely.

#### How to Identify Your ONE Thing

Ask yourself: "If the reader forgets everything else but remembers one thing, what should it be?"

Write that down. That's your headline. Everything else in the communication must support or contextualize that one thing. Nothing more.

**Example:**
- ❌ (Multiple ideas) "We're implementing a new CRM system, which will require three days of training, improve our data quality, and let us track customer interactions better."
- ✅ (One idea) "We're implementing a new CRM system on March 15."

The reader now has ONE clear fact. Supporting details (training schedule, benefits, etc.) follow as context, not as competing ideas.

### 2. Lead With the Most Important Thing

This is the inverted pyramid applied to every written format. **Most writers bury the lede.** They explain the journey, the context, the backstory - and only after 3-4 paragraphs reveal what they actually want to say.

Smart Brevity demands immediate disclosure of the single most important fact.

#### The "So What?" Test

For any opening sentence, ask: "So what?" If your opening doesn't answer that question, you haven't led with the most important thing.

**Example:**

❌ **Before (buries the lede):** "Over the past six months, our team has been exploring opportunities to enhance our customer communication strategy. We've conducted extensive research, reviewed industry best practices, and surveyed 200 customers. Based on these findings, we've decided to implement a new customer feedback system."

✅ **After (leads with importance):** "We're implementing a new customer feedback system on April 1. Here's why it matters."

The reader immediately knows the decision and its timeline. Everything else is supporting detail.

### 3. The 1-2-3 Structure (Axios Model)

Every piece of Smart Brevity communication follows this architecture:

#### **Part 1: What Matters** (The Headline + The Most Important Thing in One Sentence)
- This is the inverted pyramid's peak.
- It's the complete story in its smallest form.
- Readers who stop here still have the essential information.

#### **Part 2: Why It Matters** (Context, Impact, Relevance)
- Why should the reader care?
- What changes because of this?
- What's at stake?
- This is where you provide urgency or relevance without editorializing.

#### **Part 3: Go Deeper** (Supporting Details, Evidence, Next Steps)
- Additional context, data, nuance.
- How to act on this information.
- This section is *optional* for the reader - it's the escape hatch for those who need more.

**The magic:** Readers can stop after Part 1 and still have the core message. Parts 2 and 3 add value without burying the lede.

### 4. Strong Verbs, No Filler

This is where Smart Brevity becomes visible in sentence-level craft.

**Words that signal weak writing:**
- Modifiers of uncertainty: "could," "may," "might," "seems," "appears," "suggests"
- Multisyllabic alternatives to simple words: "utilize" instead of "use," "facilitate" instead of "help"
- Fog words: "very," "quite," "rather," "somewhat," "kind of"
- Passive voice: "The decision was made" instead of "We decided"
- Corporate jargon: "synergy," "leverage," "drill down," "circle back"

**Examples of strengthening:**

❌ "It might be helpful to consider implementing a system that could potentially improve efficiency."
✅ "We will implement a system to save 5 hours per week."

❌ "The company has come to a place where it believes collaboration is something we should pursue."
✅ "We're prioritizing collaboration."

❌ "Several factors contributed to the situation wherein we were able to achieve positive outcomes."
✅ "Three actions drove our success: [list them]."

**Subject-Verb-Object rhythm is strongest:**
"We launched the product." "Sales increased 40%." "The team adjusted strategy."

### 5. Formatting as a First-Class Tool

In Smart Brevity, formatting is not decoration. **It is structure. It is meaning. It is architecture.**

#### Bold Text as a Second Headline
Every bolded phrase should be readable as a mini-headline that stands alone. If you can read all the bold text and understand the piece, you've formatted correctly.

**Example:**
"**Sales increased 15% this quarter.** This growth was driven by three factors. **First, our new pricing model expanded our addressable market.** Second, marketing campaigns reached 200k new prospects. **Third, customer retention improved to 92%.** Going forward, we'll continue this trajectory by investing in product development."

#### Bullet Points vs. Prose
- Use bullets when listing 3+ related items
- Each bullet should be a complete thought or a consistent parallel structure
- Bullets increase scan speed by 30-40%
- Prose is stronger for narrative, explanation, or emotional content

#### White Space as Signal
- Short paragraphs (1-3 sentences) feel faster
- Generous margins and line spacing reduce cognitive load
- Readers unconsciously interpret white space as "this is important enough to stand alone"

#### Visual Hierarchy Builds Understanding
- Headline (largest)
- Subheadings (medium)
- Bold emphases (within paragraphs)
- Body text (smallest)
- This cascading structure lets readers choose their depth of engagement.

### 6. The "Smart" in Smart Brevity

This is the distinction that separates Smart Brevity from mere brevity.

**Brevity alone can be reductive.** You can strip down writing so far that you lose essential meaning. "Sales up. Good." is brief but useless.

**Smart brevity is brief AND dense with value.** Every word earns its place.

This means:
- Cut filler, never cut substance
- Remove explanation that a reader can infer; keep explanation that only you can provide
- Assume reader intelligence; don't assume reader knowledge
- The "Go Deeper" section should exist - some readers need it - but the core message must stand alone

**Example of dumb brevity vs. smart brevity:**

❌ **Dumb (just short):** "Budget cut 20%. Adjust accordingly."

✅ **Smart (short AND clear):** "We're cutting department budgets by 20% due to Q4 revenue miss. You have 30 days to replan. Priorities: protect customer delivery, preserve core team, eliminate discretionary spend."

The second version is actually longer but denser with meaning. It's "smart" because every word serves purpose.

## The Axios Writing Formula in Detail

Axios built an entire news organization around this formula. It's visible in every article, every headline, every update.

### The Headline (10 Words or Fewer)

**Function:** Teach readers something new in the shortest readable span.

**Rules:**
1. **Must be specific.** "Company announces new policy" is generic. "Walmart cuts prices on groceries starting tomorrow" teaches.
2. **Must be honest.** No clickbait. Readers see through it and distrust you long-term.
3. **Must use strong words.** "Soars," "crashes," "halts," "expands" signal movement. "Adjusts," "refines," "considers" signal hesitation.
4. **Must be scannable.** 59% of readers share articles without clicking the link. Your headline must be complete journalism in itself.

**Examples:**

❌ "Healthcare Updates" → Nobody learns anything
✅ "Healthcare hiring is recession-proof" → Specific, surprising, complete thought

❌ "Big earnings surprise" → Which direction? How big?
✅ "Apple earnings beat expectations; stock rises 5%" → Full story in 8 words

### The First Sentence (The Complete Story)

This sentence must contain the entire story in its simplest form. Someone who reads nothing else should walk away understanding what happened.

**The formula:** Subject + Verb + Most Important Fact

**Examples:**

❌ "There are several reasons why the meeting was rescheduled."
✅ "We rescheduled tomorrow's leadership meeting to next Thursday."

❌ "New technology is changing how companies approach data management."
✅ "Databricks announced a $10 billion valuation today, signaling enterprise demand for AI-powered data tools."

### The Why It Matters Paragraph

This is where you provide context without editorializing. Answer:
- Why should anyone care?
- What changes because of this?
- Who is affected?

**Common sub-headers for this section:**
- Why it matters
- The big picture
- What's next
- Catch up quick
- The stakes
- The backstory

**Example:**

"Why it matters: This valuation reflects corporate urgency to build AI infrastructure before competitors do. Data teams now have budget to modernize stacks that haven't changed in 5+ years."

### Supporting Points (Bullets or Short Paragraphs)

Each point should:
- Stand alone as a complete thought
- Build on the previous point without being dependent on it
- Use consistent parallel structure (if listing bullets)
- Be scannable in 3-5 seconds

**Example structure:**
- Point 1: The announcement
- Point 2: The data/evidence
- Point 3: The implication
- Point 4: The next step

### The Bottom Line

Often called the "so what"-this is where you resolve the story. What should the reader *do* with this information?

**Examples:**
- "The bottom line: If you manage customer data, you're about to have budget approved for tools you've wanted for years."
- "Go deeper: [Link to detailed analysis] if you want to understand the technical specifications."

## Attention Science Underpinning the Framework

Smart Brevity isn't theoretical. It's built on 40+ years of cognitive science research.

### Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller, 1988)

**Core finding:** People have limited working memory (roughly 7 items or 30 seconds of processing capacity). When you exceed this, comprehension collapses.

**How Smart Brevity applies this:**
- One main idea (doesn't exceed working memory limit)
- Breaking text into chunks (bullets, paragraphs, white space)
- Leading with the most important item (gets into memory first)
- Supporting details separated from main idea (optional for processing)

**Practical implication:** A 300-word Smart Brevity message is remembered better than a 500-word traditional message because it doesn't overload working memory.

### The F-Pattern (Nielsen Norman Group, 2006)

Eye-tracking studies show readers scan digital text in an F-shape:
1. Horizontal scan across the top (headline, first line)
2. Vertical scan down the left margin (bullet points, bolded text, subheadings)
3. A secondary horizontal scan across the middle section

**How Smart Brevity applies this:**
- Headlines at the top (caught by first horizontal scan)
- Bolded key phrases on the left side of paragraphs (caught by vertical scan)
- Subheadings that guide the eye downward
- Short lines that force multiple eye fixations

### Serial Position Effect (Ebbinghaus, 1885)

People remember the first item (primacy effect) and last item (recency effect) in a list. Middle items are forgotten.

**How Smart Brevity applies this:**
- Puts the most important fact first (primacy)
- Breaks up sections so every point has a "first" and "last" position
- Ends with a call to action or key takeaway (recency)

### Average Reading Time (Microsoft Attention Study, 2015)

- Average attention span: 8 seconds
- Average email open rate: determined by 37-50 seconds of engagement
- Average web page dwell time: 37-50 seconds
- Article completion rate for pieces over 1000 words: <10%
- Article completion rate for pieces under 300 words: >60%

**How Smart Brevity applies this:**
- Ensures core message is consumable in 8 seconds (the headline + first sentence)
- Entire update fits in 37-50 seconds at normal reading speed
- Targets 200-400 words as the sweet spot for completion
- Assumes most readers will scan, not read thoroughly

### Chunking (Miller, 1956)

"The Magical Number Seven" research shows people can hold 5-9 items in working memory. Breaking information into chunks (not individually) improves this.

**How Smart Brevity applies this:**
- Groups related facts into bullets rather than writing one long paragraph
- Uses subheadings to chunk content into logical sections
- Separates "what" from "why" from "next steps"

## Diagnosis: Identifying Smart Brevity Violations

Use this checklist to audit any piece of writing - your own or others'.

### Symptom: "I don't know what this is about"

**Diagnosis:** Multiple ideas competing for attention OR lede is buried

**Fix:** Identify the ONE most important fact. Make it the headline and first sentence. Everything else supports it.

### Symptom: "I had to read this three times to understand"

**Diagnosis:** Too much context before the point. Writer is explaining the journey instead of the destination.

**Fix:** Reorder. Put the main point first. Use "why it matters" to provide context that explains the point's importance, not before.

### Symptom: "I don't feel like reading this"

**Diagnosis:** Opening sentence doesn't teach anything. No sense of urgency or relevance.

**Fix:** Rewrite opening to promise value immediately. "We're cutting budgets" is more compelling than "Budget planning update."

### Symptom: "I got lost halfway through"

**Diagnosis:** No structure. The piece reads like a stream of consciousness rather than a clear hierarchy.

**Fix:** Add subheadings. Use bullets for lists. Bold key phrases. Give the reader visual landmarks.

### Symptom: "What am I supposed to do with this?"

**Diagnosis:** Missing a call to action or "next step." The piece ends without resolving.

**Fix:** Add a "Go Deeper," "Next Steps," or "Bottom Line" section that tells the reader what happens next.

### Symptom: "This doesn't sound like the writer"

**Diagnosis:** Corporate jargon masking authentic voice. Passive voice. Fog words.

**Fix:** Replace with simple, active language. Read it aloud - if you wouldn't say it in conversation, rewrite it.

### Symptom: "I could skip the first three paragraphs and still understand"

**Diagnosis:** Long context/explanation before the point. This is the classic "burying the lede."

**Fix:** Move the key fact to the opening. Use the context sections to explain *why* it matters, not what it is.

## Application by Content Type

Smart Brevity isn't a single format. It's a principle applied across formats. Here's how:

### Internal Memos and Slack Messages

**Structure:**
1. **Subject line (Slack headline or memo title):** The decision or announcement in 6 words
2. **First line:** The complete action in one sentence
3. **Why it matters:** 1-2 sentences explaining impact
4. **Details:** Bullets if there are 3+ supporting facts
5. **Next steps:** What the reader should do

**Example:**

Subject: Quarterly budget allocation finalized

We're implementing the new budget allocation effective April 1, increasing marketing spend 25% and reducing admin overhead 10%.

Why it matters: This reflects our strategic shift to customer acquisition and operational efficiency.

What you need to do:
- Update project budgets in Workday by March 25
- Notify your teams of the changes
- Contact Finance if you have questions

---

### Executive Communications

**Structure:**
1. **Subject line + headline:** The executive insight (not the activity)
2. **One-sentence summary:** The strategic implication
3. **Supporting data:** 3-5 key facts with interpretation
4. **Strategic implication:** How this affects the company's direction
5. **Action required:** What leadership should decide or do

**Example:**

Subject: Q3 customer retention hit 92%

Our retention rate improved 8 percentage points this quarter, indicating stronger product-market fit and reduced churn risk.

Why this matters:
- Retention improvement was driven by three factors: pricing alignment, product improvements, and customer success investments
- This removes our biggest revenue risk from the forecast
- We can now focus on expansion and new customer acquisition

The implication: We've stabilized the business. Next quarter, we can accelerate growth spending.

What we need: Board approval on the Q4 growth budget increase we discussed.

---

### Email Newsletters

**Structure:**
1. **Subject line:** The reader benefit (not "Newsletter - March 2025")
2. **Opening (2-3 sentences):** What's new or important
3. **Section 1: What matters most**
   - Headline
   - One-sentence summary
   - Why it matters (3-5 sentences)
4. **Section 2-3: Supporting stories** (same structure, briefer)
5. **Closing:** What to do next or where to go deeper

**Length target:** 300-500 words total

**Example (opening section):**

**Subject:** Why AI just became your competitive advantage

This week, OpenAI's latest model release suggests enterprise AI is moving faster than anyone expected. Here's why you should care.

**AI enterprise adoption enters the mainstream**: Anthropic released Claude 4, and access to enterprise AI went from "rare luxury" to "table stakes." Companies that don't integrate AI into their workflows in the next 6 months will fall behind competitors that did.

Why it matters: This is no longer about "exploring AI." It's about surviving with it. The advantage goes to teams that:
- Understand their AI use cases
- Build workflows around AI capabilities
- Train teams on AI tools
- Measure impact

---

### LinkedIn Posts

**Structure:**
1. **Hook (first line):** Surprising fact or question that stops scroll
2. **Setup (1-2 sentences):** Context for why this matters
3. **Main insight:** The one big idea (can be a story, a framework, a data point)
4. **Implication:** What this means for the reader
5. **Call to action:** Follow, comment, or share

**Length target:** 120-280 words (LinkedIn's sweet spot)

**Example:**

Most companies measure employee engagement with annual surveys. That's why they're always surprised by departures.

I learned this the hard way. We had 92% satisfaction scores. Then lost 20% of our team in 12 months.

The problem: Surveys measure historical satisfaction. They don't predict future departures. People leave for reasons that emerge between surveys.

Smart companies measure engagement weekly - not annually. One question, two minutes, massive signal.

"How energized do you feel about your work this week?" (1-10 scale)

We implemented this. When someone's score dropped below 6, their manager checked in. We caught 80% of departure risk before it became resignation.

Engagement isn't an HR metric. It's a leadership practice.

What does your team actually measure?

### Marketing Copy / Landing Pages

**Structure:**
1. **Headline:** The benefit, not the product
2. **Subheading:** The proof or specificity of that benefit
3. **Opening paragraph:** Why anyone should care (the problem you solve)
4. **Value props (bullets):** Three core benefits with a concrete result
5. **Social proof:** A testimonial or metric
6. **Call to action:** Specific, benefit-driven

**Example:**

Headline: Reduce email response time by 70%

Subheading: Without hiring more customer service reps

Opening: Your support team spends 15 hours a week writing the same responses. That's 780 hours a year on repetitive work.

Value Props:
- Save 10 hours per week with smart reply templates trained on your brand voice
- Respond to customers in 15 minutes instead of 2 hours
- Free up your team to focus on complex issues that actually require thinking

Proof: "We reduced response time from 4 hours to 20 minutes. Our CSAT scores improved from 78% to 94%." - VP Customer Success, Tech Company

CTA: Start your free trial. No credit card. See results in 3 days.

---

### Press Releases / Announcements

**Structure:**
1. **Headline:** The newsworthy fact (not "Company announces...")
2. **First paragraph:** Who, what, when, where, why in one paragraph
3. **Quote:** From a principal at the company (adds credibility and voice)
4. **Supporting details:** 2-3 paragraphs with relevant context, metrics, or implications
5. **Boilerplate:** Standard company description (required for media)

**Example:**

Headline: Acme Software Cuts Implementation Time by 60%, Starts Shipping to Enterprises Next Month

SAN FRANCISCO, CA - March 22, 2025 - Acme Software announced its 2.0 platform, which reduces enterprise implementation from 12 weeks to 5 weeks. The product launches publicly April 15.

"We spent three years listening to enterprise customers complain about implementation timelines," said Jane Chen, CEO of Acme. "This release directly addresses their top pain point. We're not just faster - we're 10x faster for complex deployments."

The new platform includes AI-powered data mapping, reducing manual configuration by 80%. Beta customers report implementation completion in 4-5 weeks, down from 10-12 weeks with the legacy system.

## The Tension Between Brevity and Completeness

This is where many writers struggle: **Does Smart Brevity mean I have to leave things out?**

The answer is no - but yes. Confusing, I know.

### The Hierarchy of Information

Smart Brevity works because it structures information hierarchically:

1. **Essential (must be in the headline and first paragraph):** The one thing the reader absolutely must know
2. **Important (should be in "why it matters"):** Context that makes the essential thing make sense
3. **Supporting (goes in "go deeper"):** Data, examples, nuance that some readers want
4. **Optional (can be archived or linked):** Detailed specifications, historical background, tangential context

**The magic:** You don't cut information - you reorder it. The headline doesn't include everything. Neither does the first paragraph. But all the information is there, structured by importance.

### When Brevity Becomes Reductive

You've gone too far if:
- The core message is unclear
- A reader can't understand the impact or next steps
- You're eliminating facts that directly contradict or complicate your main point
- You're omitting context that changes the meaning

**Example of reductive:** "Sales are down." (Is this bad? A seasonal pattern? A missed opportunity? We don't know.)

**Example of Smart Brevity:** "Sales are down 8% month-over-month due to the delayed product launch. This is recoverable; we're back on track for quarterly targets."

### The "Go Deeper" Principle

The secret to Smart Brevity working at scale is **embracing the optional section.** Not everyone needs complete information. But those who do need it accessible.

**This means:**
- Your main message is complete without "Go Deeper"
- "Go Deeper" doesn't change the main message; it contextualizes it
- Readers can choose their depth of engagement
- Nobody feels shortchanged because the detail is there if they want it

## Interaction with Other Writing Frameworks

Smart Brevity doesn't contradict other writing frameworks. It complements them.

### Smart Brevity + Gary Provost's Rhythm

Gary Provost taught that writing rhythm (varying sentence length) keeps readers engaged. Smart Brevity adds structure to that rhythm.

**Example of rhythm + brevity:**
"Sales increased 15%. Here's why. Three factors drove growth. First, our pricing model improved margins. Second, marketing reached new segments. Third, retention hit 92%-an all-time high."

Short sentences with variation. Scan-friendly. Rhythmic when read aloud.

### Smart Brevity + Made to Stick (Heath Brothers)

"Made to Stick" teaches six principles: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Story.

Smart Brevity is the **structure** that makes these principles land. A "Made to Stick" story that buries the lede fails. A "Made to Stick" insight that's vague isn't sticky.

Smart Brevity provides the clarity these principles need.

### Smart Brevity + StoryBrand (Donald Miller)

StoryBrand teaches positioning: customer as hero, you as guide, problem, plan, outcome.

Smart Brevity is how you execute StoryBrand in **every communication**, not just marketing. Email? Apply StoryBrand + Smart Brevity. Internal memo? Same structure.

### Smart Brevity + Jobs to Be Done (Clayton Christensen)

JTBD teaches that customers hire products to do a job. Smart Brevity is how you articulate that job clearly.

Instead of: "Acme is a cloud-based project management platform with collaboration tools and real-time visibility."

JTBD + Smart Brevity: "Acme helps teams ship projects 20% faster by eliminating status meeting overhead."

## Anti-Patterns and Failure Modes

### The Bullet-Point Soup

This is what happens when writers use bullets without discipline:

❌ **Bad:**
- We launched the product
- Customer response was positive
- Sales teams are excited
- We're planning to expand to Europe
- Training will begin next month
- Budget is allocated
- Timeline is aggressive

**Problem:** These aren't parallel. Some are actions, some are observations, some are plans. Scanning this doesn't orient the reader.

✅ **Good:**
We launched the product to positive customer response.

What we're doing next:
- Expand to European markets in Q2
- Train sales team on new features (March 15-17)
- Allocate budget for scaling ($500k)

---

### The Hollow Boldface

This is when writers bold everything, thinking it adds emphasis. Instead, it removes emphasis.

❌ **Bad:**
"**We are** implementing a **new policy** that will **impact** your **workflow.** This **change** is designed to **improve** **efficiency.** Please **review** the **guidelines** and **let us know** if you have **questions.**"

✅ **Good:**
"**We're implementing a new expense policy March 1.** This change simplifies approval workflows. Please review the guidelines and let us know if you have questions."

Only bold the one thing you want the reader to take away.

### Cutting the Wrong Things

This is when writers misunderstand Smart Brevity and remove essential context:

❌ **Cut too much:**
"Budget is cut 20%." (Reader has no context. Is this normal? A crisis? A strategic shift?)

✅ **Smart Brevity:**
"Due to Q4 revenue miss, we're cutting department budgets by 20%. This is temporary; we expect to restore in Q2. Protect customer delivery; preserve core team; eliminate discretionary spend."

---

### The Cold, Abrupt Tone

Not all brevity sounds good. Sometimes it sounds cold.

❌ **Abrupt:**
"Your project is canceled. Move to Assignment B. Questions? Email your manager."

✅ **Brief + Human:**
"We're pausing Project X due to market changes. This isn't a reflection on your work - you'll move to Assignment B starting Monday, which aligns better with our Q2 priorities. Let's talk about what this means for your role."

Brevity doesn't mean no empathy. Use short sentences, but include human context.

## Extended Before/After Examples

### Example 1: Product Announcement Email

❌ **Before (Weak):**

Subject: Product Update

Hi everyone,

We've been working on some exciting improvements to our platform. Over the past few months, our product team has been listening to customer feedback and identifying areas where we could make the experience better. We've made significant investments in our infrastructure and design.

The result is a faster, more intuitive platform that we're excited to roll out. We believe this will help your workflows and improve productivity. The new version includes several enhancements, including improved search, better navigation, and faster load times.

Thanks for your continued support.

Best,
Product Team

**Issues:** Multiple ideas (speed, design, features, maintenance), buries the announcement, vague benefits, unclear action items.

✅ **After (Smart Brevity):**

We're shipping a faster version of our platform on April 1. Load times drop 60%. Search is instant. Everything else stays the same.

**Why it matters:**
You get the same tools you love, just faster. This frees up time you spent waiting for pages to load. For teams processing large datasets, the speed gain is significant.

- Your data and settings carry over automatically
- Getting started guide: [link]

### Example 2: Internal Memo

❌ **Before (Weak):**

Memo: Budget Adjustments

To: All Department Heads

As we move into Q2, we need to adjust our spending to align with updated revenue projections. The economic environment has shifted, and our forecast for the year requires us to be more mindful of our expenditures. After careful review of our budget allocations across departments, we've determined that we need to make some changes.

Starting April 1st, all departments should plan for a 15% reduction in discretionary spending. This includes travel, consulting, software licenses, and office expenses. Non-discretionary spending (salaries, committed contracts, customer delivery) will remain stable.

The goal is to improve our margin profile while maintaining our ability to serve customers effectively. We'll review the impact in Q3 and adjust as needed based on market conditions.

**Issues:** Buries the actual decision, vague timeline, unclear what's discretionary vs. non-discretionary, creates anxiety without clarity.

## Checklist: Is This Smart Brevity?

Use this before hitting send on any important communication:

- [ ] **Can I say the main point in one sentence? If not, I have too many ideas.**
- [ ] **Does the headline teach the reader something new? (Not "Update," not "Announcement")**
- [ ] **Is the first sentence the complete story in simplest form?**
- [ ] **Does "why it matters" explain impact without editorializing?**
- [ ] **Are there any fog words? (could, may, seems, very, rather)**
- [ ] **Is the longest sentence under 20 words?**
- [ ] **Could I remove the introduction and the piece still makes sense?**
- [ ] **Would a reader who skims just the bold text understand the key point?**
- [ ] **Did I bold only one message (not everything)?**
- [ ] **Did I use bullets only for 3+ related items?**
- [ ] **Is there a clear "next step" or "go deeper" section?**
- [ ] **Would I say this sentence the same way in a conversation? If not, rewrite.**
- [ ] **Does every paragraph earn its place, or is it explanation/context that could go in "why it matters"?**

If you can check all 13 boxes, you're writing Smart Brevity.

## The Bottom Line

Smart Brevity isn't about writing less. It's about **respecting your reader's time by organizing information by importance, not by narrative flow.**

It works because:
1. Readers scan; you give them landmarks
2. Working memory is limited; you give them one idea at a time
3. Trust is built through clarity; you remove fog
4. Urgency is signaled through structure; you lead with the important thing

The framework is simple. The discipline is hard. But the result - messages that are read, understood, remembered, and acted upon - is worth the effort.
