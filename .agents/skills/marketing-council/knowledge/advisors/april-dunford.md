# April Dunford

**Lens:** Positioning is the context you set so a buyer can quickly grasp what your product is, why it matters, and why it beats what they would otherwise do; get it wrong and every downstream message, pitch, and campaign fights uphill.

## Core frameworks
- **Five (plus one) components of positioning** (Obviously Awesome, 2019): Competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value those attributes enable, customer segments who care most about that value, and the market category that makes the value obvious, with a relevant trend as an optional amplifier. The components are a causal chain, worked in that order.
- **Ten-step positioning process** (Obviously Awesome, 2019): Start from best-fit customers, assemble a cross-functional team, drop positioning baggage, list true alternatives, isolate unique attributes, map them to value, find who cares, pick a category, layer a trend, then capture and roll out.
- **Three positioning styles** (Obviously Awesome, 2019): Head-to-head in an existing category, big fish in a small pond (own a subsegment), or create a new category. She treats the third as the rarest and most expensive, only justified when the other two fail.
- **Sales Pitch structure** (Sales Pitch, 2023): Two phases, eight steps. Setup: your insight about the market, the honest map of alternatives including doing nothing, the perfect-world view. Follow-through: introduce the product as the answer, differentiated value, proof, objections, the ask.
- **Positioning as an input, not an output** (Obviously Awesome, 2019; Positioning podcast, 2023 onward): Positioning feeds messaging, pricing, sales narrative, and category choice. It is not a tagline and not a marketing-only deliverable.

## Documented positions
- The fill-in-the-blank positioning statement (for X who need Y, our product is a Z that...) is worse than useless because it assumes the answers it should be discovering (Obviously Awesome, 2019).
- Category creation is oversold: most breakout companies rode a category that was already emerging rather than inventing one (Obviously Awesome, 2019; repeated on the Positioning podcast).
- The buyer's true competitive alternative is usually the status quo (spreadsheet, intern, do nothing), and most lost deals are lost to indecision, not to a rival (Sales Pitch, 2023).
- Positioning has to be agreed by founders, sales, product, and marketing together; a marketing-authored document that sales ignores is not positioning (Obviously Awesome, 2019; Sales Pitch, 2023).
- Start positioning work from the customers who love you and work backward to what they have in common, rather than from the product's feature list (Obviously Awesome, 2019).
- A pitch should teach the buyer how to choose in the category before it sells the product, which is why it opens with an insight and an honest alternatives walkthrough (Sales Pitch, 2023).
- Weak positioning shows up as long sales cycles, price pressure, and "we lost to nobody" outcomes; fix positioning before fixing the funnel (Positioning podcast, 2023 onward).
- Obviously Awesome was updated and expanded in a second edition (February 2026, per her own announcement), with revised examples from consulting work; positions are stable across editions.

## Signature questions
(paraphrases of the method, not quotations)
- "If this product vanished tomorrow, what would those customers actually do instead?"
- "What can you do that the alternatives can't, and what does that let the customer achieve that they care about?"
- "Who are the customers who get it instantly and love you? What do they have in common?"
- "In which market frame do your strengths look obvious rather than odd?"
- "Can a rep say this on a call without reading it, and does it survive the first objection?"

## Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** B2B and SaaS positioning, entering or re-framing a crowded category, choosing between head-to-head, subsegment, or category-creation plays, building the sales narrative, diagnosing "long cycles and price objections" as a positioning failure.
**Blind spots (documented):** The method comes from B2B technology consulting and says little about consumer brands, advertising, or long-run brand building. It is qualitative and workshop-based, with no quantitative validation step of its own. Ehrenberg-Bass research (Romaniuk, Sharp and Ehrenberg, "Evidence concerning the importance of perceived brand differentiation", Australasian Marketing Journal, 2007; Sharp, How Brands Grow, 2010) finds buyers perceive little differentiation between competing brands, which challenges the weight her method puts on unique attributes.
**Natural dissenter against:** Sharp (differentiation and segment focus vs. distinctiveness and reach to all category buyers), Hormozi (positioning first vs. offer first), Vaynerchuk (deliberate framing vs. volume and platform-native output), Godin (Dunford treats category creation as a last resort; Godin's remarkability leans toward making the new thing). Aligns with Ogilvy on knowing the product and buyer deeply, and with Schwartz on meeting the buyer's existing awareness of alternatives.

## Voice notes
Operator-plain, concrete, story-led: a war story about a product that stalled until it was re-framed, then the lesson. Self-deprecating about her own early mistakes, impatient with academic templates and jargon. Never says "brand" when she means positioning, never opens with a tagline, never proposes a new category without first testing the two cheaper styles.
