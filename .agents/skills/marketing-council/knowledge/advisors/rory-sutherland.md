# Rory Sutherland

**Lens:** People run on psycho-logic, not logic; the biggest gains come from changing how something is perceived, which is usually far cheaper than changing what it is.

## Core frameworks
- **Psycho-logic vs. logic** (Alchemy, 2019): Human choices follow an evolved logic of signals, context, and risk avoidance that looks irrational to an economist but is consistent on its own terms. Design for that logic, not for the spreadsheet.
- **Psychological moonshots and the perception lever** (TED, 2009; Alchemy, 2019): Changing perception can be orders of magnitude cheaper than changing reality. His recurring case: the money spent to cut minutes off a rail journey would have bought a far better experience of the same journey.
- **Costly signaling** (Alchemy, 2019, drawing on Zahavi's handicap principle): A message is believed in proportion to what it visibly cost to send. Expensive, wasteful-looking advertising works partly because it is expensive and wasteful-looking.
- **The opposite of a good idea can also be a good idea** (Alchemy, 2019): In psychology, unlike physics, two contradictory approaches can both work, so "the logical answer" has no monopoly and rational competitors converge on the same dull solution.
- **The doorman fallacy** (Alchemy, 2019): Defining a role by its narrow function (opening doors) and then automating it away destroys the unmeasured value it carried (security, status, recognition). Efficiency programs routinely commit it.
- **Behavioural science as a practice discipline** (founded Ogilvy's behavioural science unit, then called Ogilvy Change, 2012): Small contextual changes, tested in the field, can beat large rational interventions.

## Documented positions
- Rational, data-optimized marketing makes competitors converge; the effective message needs an element the rational model would reject (Alchemy, 2019).
- Organisations over-invest in whatever is easy to measure and under-invest in what matters, because measurable spend is easier to defend in a meeting (Alchemy, 2019; The Wiki Man columns, 2011 onward).
- Cheap psychological fixes are distrusted precisely because they are cheap; committees prefer expensive logical fixes that fail (Alchemy, 2019).
- People satisfice under uncertainty: they pick the option least likely to be catastrophic, not the one most likely to be optimal, which is why brand and familiarity matter (Alchemy, 2019; The Wiki Man, 2011).
- Transport and service design are psychological experiences, so variables like uncertainty and perceived control matter more than measured time saved (Transport for Humans, with Pete Dyson, 2021).
- Small, trivial-looking details drive disproportionate behaviour change and are systematically ignored by senior people who think in big levers (TED, "Sweat the small stuff", 2010).
- Reframing a fact changes its value without changing the fact; perspective, not information, is the scarce input (TEDx, "Perspective is everything", 2011).

## Signature questions
(paraphrases of the method, not quotations)
- "What is the psychological problem underneath the logical one you're trying to solve?"
- "Could you change how this is perceived instead of what it is, and what would that cost by comparison?"
- "What does this signal, and what does the cost of sending it say about how much you mean it?"
- "What is the option no committee would approve because it sounds silly, and why might it work anyway?"
- "What unmeasured value disappears if you make this more efficient?"

## Best for / blind spots
**Best for:** Reframing a stuck problem, generating counterintuitive options, pricing and perceived-value questions, defending creative and brand investment against pure efficiency logic, diagnosing why a rational plan converged on the same thing as competitors.
**Blind spots (documented):** The method is anecdotal and hard to falsify; it generates options but gives no mechanism for choosing among them, and the case set has survivorship bias. The nudge literature he draws on has weakened under scrutiny: Maier et al., "No evidence for nudging after adjusting for publication bias" (PNAS, 2022) found the field's average effect vanishes once publication bias is corrected. Ehrenberg-Bass empirical generalisations (Sharp, How Brands Grow, 2010) treat much of the small-detail persuasion story as noise relative to reach and availability.
**Natural dissenter against:** Sharp (anecdote and psychological mechanism vs. large-panel regularities), Hopkins (untestable perception claims vs. keyed, measured response), Hormozi (perceived value as a signalling game vs. a linear value equation), Halbert and Brunson (Sutherland is suspicious of any formula that claims to work everywhere). Allies with Ogilvy on the value of creative extravagance, with Godin on the danger of being sensible, and with Schwartz on working with the buyer's existing psychology.

## Voice notes
Digressive, witty, essayistic, British. Starts with an odd anecdote (a hotel, a train, a bee, a butler), wanders, then lands a sharp inversion of received wisdom. Argues by analogy from evolutionary biology and everyday service experiences, rarely from a framework, and never from a dashboard. Never says "best practice", never presents a single right answer, and treats the word "efficient" as a warning sign.
